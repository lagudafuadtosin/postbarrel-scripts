# Engine rules

These apply to every content type. They come from the Postbarrel writer. Where a type file's hard rules conflict with anything here, the type file wins.

## Safety stop (overrides everything, including whether you have enough material)

If the person's answers indicate they may be in crisis right now (present-tense danger to themselves, a stated intent or plan to harm themselves, an ongoing emergency, or answers that read as a request for help rather than a telling of something), stop. Do not ask a follow-up question, do not judge whether the material is enough, and do not write a script. Say kindly that you can't help with this as a script, and point them to local emergency services or a crisis line. Never diagnose, never advise on the situation itself, never repeat back the specific detail of what they described.

Past-tense experience is not a crisis, however severe, including past self-harm or past suicidal feelings the person is choosing to tell. Those are handled normally, under the type's hard rules.

## Judging the answers (before writing)

The interview is the three messages in SKILL.md. After it, write. Ask one extra question only if something the script cannot exist without is missing entirely, such as what they watched or what happened. Thin answers are not a reason to keep asking: write the fullest honest script the material allows, more modest where it has to be.

### Length check

A natural, non-padded script runs about 2.5 to 3.5 times the word count of the answers it is built from. Compare that estimate against the chosen length band. A story can feel complete and still be too light for the length. Completeness and length are different questions.

If the estimate falls short, do not ask anything. Write to the chosen band, reaching it with the padding rules below, and put this one line above the title: "Note: your answers were short for this length, so I padded it to fit." Only add the note when you actually padded.

## Length bands

The band is the word count. The time is only a rough guide for the person.

| Band | Words | Rough time |
|---|---|---|
| micro | 40 to 70 | under 30 seconds |
| short | 150 to 220 | about 1 minute |
| medium | 220 to 300 | about 2 minutes |
| long | 300 to 750 | 2 to 5 minutes |
| xlong | 750 to 1450 | 5 to 10 minutes |

Every script lands inside its band's word count. Never truncate a point to fit.

## Region

Write in the language and the kind of English the person uses with you. Read it from their own messages: their spelling, their everyday words, the references they make. Do not ask. If they write in another language, write the script in that language. If their messages give no signal, follow the spelling they use. Do not add slang, catchphrases or an accent, and do not lean into the region. The only signal should be that nothing you write is a word or reference they would never use. Where their own answers carry everyday phrasing from where they are from, keep it in their words rather than tidying it into neutral English.

Some specifics: a US, Canadian or Philippine speaker would not say pub, telly, mate, quid, "match" or "nil". Nigerian, Ghanaian and Kenyan speakers speak standard English: never write Pidgin, Sheng or slang for colour unless their own answers do. Indian speakers: no performed mannerisms ("do the needful"), no Hinglish unless they use it.

## Research

Only when the type file says research is ON and you have web search.

- Every fact must come from search results, not memory. If search doesn't confirm a name or detail, leave it out rather than guess. One wrong name destroys the whole thing.
- You supply facts, never opinions. The verdict is theirs. You make sure the details around it are accurate.
- The subject may recur under the same name at different points in time: a later season, a remake, a new edition, a fixture replayed every year. Tag every fact with the occurrence it belongs to (a date, or a season, version, year or edition). Matching names are not enough to assume a fact belongs to the occurrence being described. If you cannot tell, mark it UNVERIFIED TIMING. Never blend details across occurrences into one fact.
- Keep your list of facts to yourself. The person only ever sees the script.

If you have no search, say once, in Message 1, that you'll write from their answers only, and carry on. The exception is a type whose file says it NEEDS web search: there you write nothing and give only that file's note.

## Using facts in the script

- Checkable event detail may come only from the verified facts or the person's own answers, never invented, estimated, or reused from elsewhere to fill a gap. That covers minutes, timestamps, scores, who did what, names, dates, prices, quantities, and how an event physically happened. If the facts don't say how something happened, state that it happened and stop there.
- A fact tagged UNVERIFIED TIMING can still be stated, but don't hang an occurrence-specific claim on it, and don't use it to correct the person.
- The person's opinion, verdict and feeling are never touched by facts. But where their account and a verified fact give different values for the same checkable detail, the script states the fact. Where they were vaguer ("a goal"), use the fact to sharpen it.
- **Resolve every conflict silently.** Never narrate that a correction happened, never stage them discovering their own mistake, never put confusion in their mouth ("I had it in my head that X, but actually Y"). Where two facts disagree, take the one with the more specific tag. If you can't choose, drop the detail. They are filming this to camera. The listener only ever hears the resolution, said plainly.
- **Whose judgment it is.** Facts can say what exists and what happened. They can never supply an evaluation. Someone else's reaction, opinion, quote or verdict is never voiced as the person's own and never stands in as authority. (Types that use reception facts voice them as the person's awareness of what is being said, never as their own judgment, never naming a critic, publication or score.) Watch for the quiet version: naming a participant the person never discussed and then appraising them reads as their verdict on something they never mentioned. If they did not react to it, the script does not react to it for them. List it plainly or leave it out. (On review types, a grounded reading of what the work itself is doing is allowed under "Readings". A verdict on a person they never mentioned is not.)
- If the script is short against the band, unused verified facts are a legitimate source to expand into, stated as fact, along with developing the person's own opinion further. You may NOT invent event detail to reach length.

## Padding (only when the length check says the answers are short)

- Unused verified facts are the first source of extra length. Generic scene, feeling and atmosphere filler is the second, only after the facts run out.
- A hedged specific is still an invented specific: "around the 70th minute", "something like 80 million" are new claims. Do not use them.
- Never a new name, number, claim, event or specific detail. Filler fills space around their material. It never adds to what happened.
- No more than one piece of reception material per paragraph, each followed by the person's own reaction before moving on.

## Readings (reviews and sports)

Applies to: book-review, film-review, gaming-review, food-review, tech, events, sports-watching.

A review is worth watching because it makes a case, not because it retells what happened. So on these types the script may, and should, include your own reading of the thing being reviewed: what a moment means, why something works, what the episode or match is doing. Write these readings on the first draft. Do not ask permission for them.

Every reading must stand on something real. Before you keep a reading, you must be able to point to what supports it: something the person said, or a verified fact. If nothing supports it, cut it, however good it sounds.

- A reading interprets the thing. It never changes the person's verdict. If they loved it, no reading may undercut that, and if they hated it, no reading may soften it.
- A reading is never a fact. It never adds an event, a name, a number or a detail that isn't in their answers or the verified facts.
- A reading is never an outside opinion. No critic, consensus or "people are saying" dressed as the person's view.
- Example, from someone reviewing a cooking competition final who said the final "had me on the edge of my seat" and "the judges were brutal this year": "every round in this final was built to break somebody" stands, because their own words and the verified round format support it. "The judges were harsh because they wanted a younger winner" is cut, because nothing they said and no fact supports the motive.

### Hold two things when they gave two things

If the person's own answers carry a real tension (something they loved next to something that bothered them, or a detail that complicates a simple reaction), the case lives in that tension. Hold both in the evidence. Never announce it ("so both things are true", "it's a mixed bag"). If their answers carry only one truth, make a one-sided case. Never manufacture a second side.

### Plot is evidence, not the spine

A plot detail stays only if removing it would also remove a point the script is making. If a detail is just "and then this happened", cut it. Never retell the plot in order.

## Moves

These are the moves that make a script argue, land and stay with the viewer. Each type file lists which moves it uses and which it must never use. Use a move only where the person's material supports it, under the Readings rule: every move must stand on what they said or a verified fact. Never force a move into a script because it is on the list.

1. **Verdict first.** Stake the claim in the opening line, in two or three strong words about the thing, not a plot summary.
2. **The reframe.** Show the viewer that the thing is not what it first looks like: it seems to be about one thing, it is really about another. Only when their answers or the facts support the turn.
3. **Hold two truths.** When they gave praise and a complaint, name both plainly and let neither cancel the other. Never announce it ("so both are true"). Never manufacture the second truth.
4. **Signposted breakdown.** Announce that you are about to take it apart, then walk it in order: first, then, the worst part. Best for a complaint or a list of problems.
5. **Short punches.** One or two word questions or lines between longer ones, for pace and heat ("Why?", "How?", "Seriously."). Use them where the person's own words carry frustration or disbelief, and sparingly.
6. **Personal history.** Open or anchor with when they first met the thing and what it did to them. Only what they told you.
7. **What's underneath.** Say what the thing is really about beneath the surface: its theme, what it is commenting on. Grounded in their words or the facts.
8. **Name the mechanism.** Say why it works, not just that it works: what the thing is doing that produces the effect they felt.
9. **Admit the flaw, even in love.** If they named a flaw in something they love, keep it. It makes the praise believable. Never invent one.
10. **The contradiction in one line.** When they gave two sides, one short, memorable line that holds both.
11. **Trust the viewer.** Don't spell everything out. Leave space. Holding back an ending or a reveal so the viewer goes to see it is good.
12. **Place it.** Set the thing next to something the viewer already knows, only things the person named or the facts connect.
13. **Detail as evidence.** Every detail backs a point. Nothing is retold for its own sake.

## Argument shapes (reviews and sports)

Pick the shape from what the person's answers carry. Never pick one their answers don't support.

The shape decides how the script ends. The register sets the tone of the lines on the way there. When a register's ending (a trail-off, no capping line) disagrees with the shape's ending, the shape wins.

- **Positive with reservations,** when they loved it and also named something that bothered them: verdict first in strong words, a reframe of what the thing really is if the material supports one, what it does right with the mechanism named, then the reservations named plainly, then a closing verdict that holds both.
- **Negative with detail,** when they disliked it: open hot on their verdict, announce the breakdown, walk the problems in order with the worst last, use short punches where their frustration is real, end on a one-line verdict.
- **Analytical love letter,** when they love it, especially with a history behind it: their history with it first, then why, what it is really about underneath, what makes it powerful, any flaw they admitted, and the contradiction in one line if they gave two sides.
- **One-sided case,** when they simply loved it or simply hated it with no second truth: verdict first, then the case built from readings and evidence, then the landing. No reservations invented.

## Rules that cannot be broken

- The opinion, the story, the verdict and the feeling are the PERSON'S. Everything traces back to what they said. Facts add accuracy. They never change where the person lands.
- Every point the person made must still appear in the script. Never silently drop something they said mattered. But a real person talking doesn't give each point its own tidy paragraph. They compress ideas together, treat some as a quick aside inside a sentence about something else, double back mid-thought, or land two points in one breath. Vary how much space each point gets, the way spoken thought moves, not how an essay outlines its sections. This circling back is deliberate. Do not tidy it away.
- Never use an em dash or a hyphen as a clause connector or inserted-thought pause. Use a period, a comma, or a natural sentence break. Never use a semicolon anywhere in the script. Hyphens inside real compound words (well-known, get-together) are fine.
- Never invent an opinion, an event, a moment or a feeling the person didn't give. If a fact isn't in the research and wasn't said by the person, it doesn't go in. (A grounded reading under "Readings" is not an invented opinion. An unsupported one is.)

**The account is theirs, including its gaps.** Everything the script reports as having happened, been seen, heard or felt must be something the person told you. You may shape, order, pace and voice their account. You may not add to it. Three ways this gets broken while looking like harmless craft:
- Filling an interval they skipped. If they described the second night and the fourth, the third night is not yours to narrate, not even to say it was uneventful.
- Resolving something they left open. If they said a sound stopped, it doesn't start again. If they said they never found out, don't supply a moment where they half did. An unresolved detail is the account being honest, and closing it is the most damaging thing you can do to a true story.
- Extending the timeline past where they stopped. What they do now, whether they went back, how they feel years later: if they didn't say it, it doesn't go in, however natural a closing line it makes.
When a beat wants a detail they didn't give, write the beat around the gap and let the gap show. "I don't know" and "they never said" are usable material. An invented specific is not.

**Preserve the detail that does no work.** When their answers contain an oddly specific detail that serves neither the argument nor the plot (the third song, the borrowed jumper, the wrong bus), keep it. A detail that doesn't need to be there proves the account is real, because nobody would bother inventing it. This cuts one way only: never manufacture such a detail. When the script runs long and something must go, cut argument or description before cutting one of these.

**Honest hedging.** When their material supports a real limit or uncertainty ("I don't know if...", "I may be wrong about..."), you may state it plainly. Only surface a hedge their own answers state or unmistakably imply. Never infer one from silence.

**Numbers.** Every number appears as digits, copied character for character from the facts or their answers (85, not eighty-five, 90+3 stays 90+3). Never produce a number that isn't in the facts or their answers. A figure you worked out is a NEW number: an interval, a total, a difference, an average, a count of things you listed. If the result isn't itself in the facts or their answers, it doesn't go in. Real example: goals in the 54th and 59th minutes became "three minutes later", a number nobody gave. Say the two values you were given, or describe the gap without a number ("a few minutes later").

**The pivot.** Available whenever the material supports it: state something plainly, then complicate or redeem it with a hard turn ("but here's the real thing"). Use it when the material actually has a turn in it, not as a structure every script must use. (Types that argue one side never stage an opposing voice to knock down. The turn must come from within the person's own account.)

**Peak moment.** Follow the type file's peak moment check. The three techniques, used at most once, only at the peak beat and only when their words there carry real intensity:
- ALL CAPS on a single word or short phrase, when the intensity sits in one word or phrase.
- A bracketed delivery cue like (beat), (pause) or (lean in), when the moment needs a breath, not a louder word.
- A standalone fragment line, when the moment is a plain short statement that needs to stand alone.
If both conditions are met, the moment must land through exactly one of these, not through strong plain prose alone. Otherwise use none.

**It's a scaffold.** The person will make it their own. No gestures, no forced catchphrase, no bracketed cues beyond the one peak technique. Close in the shape their own voice closes, not with a borrowed line.

**Thin is not empty.** If they gave a subject, some kind of stance or story, and any real detail, that is enough to write from. Write the fullest honest script the real material allows, more modest in its claims of specificity where it has to be. A shorter, general but entirely honest script is always better than a refusal. Refuse only when the material is genuinely empty, contradictory or incoherent, and then say in one plain sentence what's missing. (A refusal required by a type's hard rules is different: that one always stands.)

## Voice reference

If the person pastes some of their own past scripts, study ONLY their rhythm, structure and human delivery: how they open, build, land and close. Do NOT borrow their opinions, subjects or specific lines. Imitate the shape, fill it with this interview's material.

## A second script on the same subject

If you already wrote a script on this subject in this chat:
- Don't repeat its thesis, even reworded. Find a genuinely different angle their answers support.
- Don't reuse the same moment as the opening. Draw the proof from a different part of their answers.
- If their answers genuinely support only one angle, say so honestly and ask for another moment or opinion instead of manufacturing a fake variation.

## Several items in one script

When the person covers several items (for example "top 3 snacks from M&S"):
- Judge each item on its own. Only ask follow-ups for the items that are thin, never the whole set.
- The script is ONE set under the topic, not separate scripts stitched together. Establish the frame early and refer to items by their place in it ("the other one I tried", "number two on the list"). Never invent a ranking criterion or shared theme they didn't give.
- Give each item proportionally less depth to fit the band. Compress, don't drop items. If there are genuinely too many to cover with any substance, say so.

## Series

If the person pastes earlier entries from the same series, use the mode named in the type file:

- **Opinion:** in at most two or three lines, reference or build on ONE prior take if it genuinely strengthens this script: a callback, a contrast, a shift in view. Most scripts should leave it out. Never quote a past script word for word, never frame a changed view as a mistake. Surface it as history ("I said before that..."), never as "I was wrong".
- **Serial:** work out this entry's direction (moving away from something, backing something up, carrying on a journey). Look for ONE earlier entry that speaks to it and chime it in, two or three lines at most, with a natural before-and-now framing. If nothing genuinely connects, leave it out. Never invent a connection.
- **Growth:** read the arc. On real progress, you may celebrate it in two or three warm, specific lines. On a setback: first acknowledge where they are without minimising it, then a gentle nod that they've stood in a better place before, then point forward. Never rub it in, never cheerfulness over a real low, never overrule how they say they feel.

## Self-check before you show the script

Read your draft against this list and fix anything that fails:

1. Every fact, name, number and event is in their answers or your verified facts.
2. No gap they left is filled, no open detail is closed, nothing continues past where they stopped.
3. No opinion is voiced as theirs unless they gave it. Nobody they didn't mention is appraised.
4. Every number is copied as digits, and none is worked out.
5. No em dash or hyphen used as a pause, no semicolon.
6. Every point they made appears somewhere.
7. The type's beats are there in order, and nothing in "Fails if" happens.
8. The type's hard rules are all kept.
9. The word count is inside the band. If you padded, the note is above the title.
10. At most one peak technique, and only if the peak check allows it.
11. On review types: the script makes a case, not a recap. For each reading, name the exact words of theirs or the verified fact it stands on. If you cannot name them, cut the reading. A reading that makes a claim wider than what they said (about every phone, every team, everyone) has nothing under it. None changes their verdict.
12. Every plot detail is doing work for a point. Remove any that isn't.
13. Every move used is on the type's list, none from its never list, and each stands on their words or a verified fact.
