# Storytime

What this is: a spoken telling of a true personal story.

## The interview

These are the questions behind the three-message interview in SKILL.md. Fold them into those messages in plain words. Optional ones can be skipped.

1. What happened? Tell it like you'd tell a friend.
   Hint for them: Start anywhere. Don't worry about order, just get the story out.
2. What was the exact moment it turned?
   Hint for them: The second everything changed, what you saw, heard, or realised. The more exact, the better.
3. How did it end, and how do you feel about it now?
   Hint for them: Where things actually landed. If it's still unresolved, say that, that's a real ending too.
4. Anything else you want to add? More detail, another angle, anything you didn't get to? (optional)
   Hint for them: Optional: Fill, if you've got more to say. Skip, if you don't.

What a strong script needs from these answers: a real event that happened to them (or that they witnessed firsthand), the turning point — the single moment where the story pivots, and where it landed — how it actually ended and how they feel about it now. Unresolved endings are allowed and real; a story does not need a neat conclusion to be tellable.

How to judge the answers: For a story, the most common gap is the middle: people give the setup and the ending but skip the escalation — how things got from normal to the turning point. If the middle is missing, ask what happened between. The second most common gap is concreteness at the peak: if the turning point is summarised ('then it all went wrong') rather than shown, ask for one exact detail from that moment — what was said word-for-word, what they physically saw, what they felt in their body. Also listen for whether the ending is genuinely resolved or genuinely unresolved — both are fine, but the script must match the truth. Never probe for more drama than the person offered; if they tell it calmly, the story is calm.

## Research

Research is OFF for this type. Nothing can be verified, so write only from the person's answers.

## Script structure

Build the script through these beats, in this order. Each rule is a requirement. "Fails if" names the mistake to avoid.

**cold_open**
- Open at or just before the most gripping point of the story — a line that opens a loop the listener needs closed. Never open with throat-clearing setup ('so basically, last year...')
- Fails if: It opens with chronological background instead of a loop, or the opening line could belong to any story rather than this one

**setup**
- Rewind and give only the context needed to understand the loop just opened — who, where, what normal looked like. Compressed: this beat earns the escalation, it is not the story
- Fails if: It includes background the payoff never uses, or it runs longer than the escalation

**escalation**
- Build from normal toward the turning point with rising tension — each sentence adds new information or raises the stakes, drawn only from what the person described. A short, sharp verbal marker — a single word or phrase repeated at each turn ('and then—', 'so—') — can mark each escalating beat landing, giving the story a rhythm the listener can feel building, if the person's own telling has that kind of punch to it.
- If the material is weak here: If the person gave no middle, bridge with the minimum honest connective ('and then, out of nowhere—') rather than inventing intermediate events they never described. When the person's own account genuinely has a gap they don't remember, the more honest and more natural rendering is often a flat admission of the gap itself — 'I don't know what happened after that' — rather than any connective bridge at all, however minimal. Prefer the flat admission when the person's own wording suggests they genuinely don't know, not just that the moment is unremarkable.
- Fails if: It invents events, escalating details, or dialogue the person never gave, or the tension flatlines because everything was revealed in the setup

**payoff** (peak beat)
- Close the loop with what actually happened, using the person's own peak moment at full force — their exact words, their exact detail. If the real ending is unresolved, land on the unresolved truth; never manufacture closure
- Fails if: It resolves the story more neatly than reality did, or the payoff is vaguer than the setup promised

**reflection**
- Optional, short: where they stand now — what it means, what they'd say to that version of themselves, or an open question they still carry. Only if q_now contains genuine reflection; otherwise end on the payoff
- Fails if: It adds a lesson, moral, or silver lining the person never expressed. Also fails if it resolves into a considered, writerly closing line ('That's it. That's the whole story') when the person's own material suggests the story just stops or trails into something else entirely — abrupt collapse is often more honest than a tidy closing beat, and is not the same failure as adding a moral or lesson.

Ordering exception: If the story's power is in comic escalation rather than a twist — an absurd situation compounding — a straight chronological telling with a strong promise-line up front ('this is the story of the worst flight of my life') can beat a cold open. Choose based on where the person's strongest sentence lives: twist stories open at the peak, escalation stories open with the promise.

Peak moment check: look at the person's own words at the "payoff" beat, wherever it ends up. If they carry real intensity (repetition, an absolute, a superlative, weight), mark it with exactly ONE of the three peak techniques in engine-rules.md, and nowhere else. If their words there are plain, use none.

## Delivery registers

Read the person's emotional register from their own words, pick the one that matches, and apply its mechanics throughout.

**SUSPENSE / DREAD**: Their telling carries fear, unease, or the sense that something was wrong before they knew what — scary encounters, near-misses, horror storytime
- Withhold — release information slower than the listener wants it, one detail at a time.
- Slow down as the turning point approaches; sentences get shorter as tension gets higher.
- Use present tense at the peak even though the event is past — 'I'm standing there, and I hear it again.'
- Let silence do work: a short flat sentence after a reveal ('Nobody else was home.') hits harder than description.
- Never explain the fear — show the detail that caused it and let the listener feel it themselves.

**COMEDY / ABSURD**: Their telling finds the situation ridiculous — disasters they can laugh at, compounding chaos, 'you won't believe this' energy
- Escalate in threes where the material allows — each beat worse than the last, using only events they actually described.
- Play it straight: deliver absurd details deadpan, as plain fact, and let the absurdity land on its own.
- Callbacks — a detail from the setup returning at the worst possible moment, if the person's story contains one.
- The narrator can be the fool: self-deprecation in their register, never invented humiliation beyond what they told.
- Short incredulous asides as their own sentences: 'And here's the thing.' 'It gets worse.'
- Deliberate tonal clash for comic effect — modern, casual language dropped into a serious or dramatic moment ('and then, I kid you not, he just said—') can heighten the absurdity, when the story's own energy supports it. Never applied to genuinely heavy or vulnerable material.
- Sound-effect-as-narration — onomatopoeia dropped directly into the telling for comic timing ('Ring, ring, ring, and then—'), when the person's own telling has that kind of texture.

**VULNERABLE / RAW**: Their telling is quiet, heavy, or careful — loss, hard seasons, things that still hurt, stories told plainly without performance
- Plain language throughout — no dramatic vocabulary, no reaching for poetry. The weight is in the facts.
- Slower rhythm, shorter sentences, more full stops than commas.
- Understatement over amplification: say less than the moment deserves and let the gap speak.
- It's allowed to be unfinished — trailing thoughts, an admission of not knowing, no tidy arc.
- Never perform pain the person didn't express; match their temperature exactly, not the topic's.

**INDIGNANT / CAN-YOU-BELIEVE-THIS**: Their telling carries outrage at unfairness — being wronged, witnessing injustice, a system or person that failed them
- State what happened plainly, then name exactly why it was wrong, using their own reasoning.
- Direct appeals to the listener as witness: 'Tell me that's normal.' 'Am I wrong?'
- Controlled burn, not rant — the outrage builds through accumulating facts, not through volume.
- Cap each wrong with a short flat line — 'And that was that.' 'No apology. Nothing.'
- Keep the receipts front and centre: dates, exact words said to them, what the rules actually were.

## Voice signature (always, on top of the register)

- Direct address that pulls the listener into the room: 'And I need you to understand—', 'You know that feeling when—', appearing mid-script, not just at the open.
- An invented listener interruption, self-answered — pose a question as if the listener just interrupted to ask it, then answer it in the same breath: 'Why do they think he did it? You asked what?' A manufactured back-and-forth, distinct from the direct address above, which speaks to the listener without inventing their side of it.
- Genuine mid-sentence backtracking is a real pivot device, not only a clean rewind phrase — a sentence that starts one way and corrects itself ('and she was— no, wait, it was her sister who—') can read truer than always reaching for a tidy transition like 'here's where it gets weird.' Short standalone pivot fragments ('So.' 'And then it happened.') are still fair game too — match whichever the person's own telling actually sounds like, not one fixed formula.
- Repetition for emphasis, not always varied rephrasing — landing on the same word or short phrase two or three times ('I knew. I knew right then. I knew.') can carry more weight than restating the point a new way each time, when the person's own telling carries that kind of intensity.
- Run-on or trailing sentences where the emotion genuinely pulls that way — a thought that keeps adding clauses because the moment is still unresolved for them, or one that trails off mid-thought, both read truer than a script that tidies every sentence into a clean landing. Only where their own telling has that quality; a calm story stays calm.
- Reconstructed dialogue as the primary narration device — telling the story through back-and-forth exchanges ('he said... she said...') rather than pure description, when the person's own telling includes any remembered dialogue, even paraphrased. This isn't limited to the story's two main figures — a minor or institutional party (police, a prosecutor, a court, 'the FBI') can get a short paraphrased reaction line too, when the person actually described that exchange. This only applies to dialogue the person actually described happening — never invent lines they didn't report, for a main figure or a minor one.
- Meta-awareness of the telling itself as a device — a self-aware acknowledgment of the video or narration's own length or structure while telling it, referencing pacing or other content out loud: 'I don't want to make this video too long.'
- Filler and hedge words as default texture, not an occasional device — 'whatever,' 'kind of,' 'sort of,' 'I think,' 'or something,' 'like,' 'you know what I mean,' 'right' woven throughout at natural density, not sprinkled in twice for flavor. This is how the sentence sounds while it's being spoken, not a stylistic garnish added after.
- Live, visible estimation rather than clean hedged phrasing — the person audibly doing the math mid-sentence and landing on a rough number: 'maybe not even 10 minutes,' 'I'm going to argue maybe another 20 minutes.' This is rougher and more specific than a tidy 'around 20 minutes' — it sounds like a guess happening in real time, not a smoothed-over approximation.
- A single word or short phrase repeated as ironic punctuation across the whole piece, doing the same flat, deflating job each time it recurs — not varied for elegance, used exactly the same way over and over ('Brilliant.' after every setback). Distinct from repetition-for-emphasis, which repeats within one moment — this repeats across the entire story as its running rhythm.
- Self-correction of the telling's own order as a live device — realising mid-script that something should have come earlier and saying so: 'oh, I should have said this first —', then supplying it. Only where it reads as natural spoken sequencing, never as a manufactured tic.

## Required in every script

the script must prove this really happened — exact words someone said, a time of day, a place detail, a physical sensation, a specific object. Concrete, sensory, verifiable-feeling specifics are what separate a true story from a written one. When only general-but-real material is available (e.g. 'we argued for hours' rather than one quoted line), use that faithfully rather than inventing false precision — generality is honest, invented detail is not.

## Hard rules for this type, above all others

- Never dramatize real harm, grief, or trauma beyond the temperature the person themselves used — the story is theirs to weight, not the script's to inflate.
- Never invent resolution, recovery, or a redemptive arc the person did not report. An unresolved true ending beats a fabricated closed one, always.
- Never add a moral, lesson, or silver lining the person didn't express.

If one of these rules requires a refusal, refuse, however rich the material is. Say plainly what you cannot write and why.
