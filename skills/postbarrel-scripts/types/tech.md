# Tech

What this is: a spoken piece about tech they actually used, switched to, built, or lived with.

This type can cover several items in one script (for example "my top 3"). If the person wants more than one, ask for the overall topic first, then ask the questions below once per item, and use the multi-item rules in engine-rules.md.

This type has a rating: offer it in message 1 ("Add a rating out of 5 or 10 if you like."). If they give one, the script may use it, on the scale they gave. Never invent one.

## The interview

These are the questions behind the three-message interview in SKILL.md. Fold them into those messages in plain words. Optional ones can be skipped.

1. What's the tech, and what have you actually done with it? include the model or generation if there's more than one.
   Hint for them: The product, app, setup, or build, and your real use: how long, for what, how much.
2. What's your take, the one thing you'd argue about it?
   Hint for them: The claim you'd defend: it's overhyped, it changed how you work, it failed you, the cheap one is better, say why.
3. The one moment in use that proves your point?
   Hint for them: The day it broke, the task it nailed, the number you actually got, the exact annoyance, the specific that settles it.

What a strong script needs from these answers: a real thing they did with tech — used a product long enough to know it, switched, built something, had something fail on them — plus a take they'd argue, and at least one specific in-use moment or spec as proof (the exact thing that happened, the number they actually hit, the day it broke). This profile openly does not do spec-sheet recitals or news explainers: no lived use, no script.

How to judge the answers: For tech, the take is their claim, the specific is one in-use moment, the verdict is keep/switch/avoid in their own terms. The most common gap is spec-talk with no life in it — reciting what the thing has instead of what happened when they used it; push for the moment: the actual day, the actual task, the actual failure. Duration of use is quiet gold — 'six months in' and 'returned it in a week' are both powerful, so surface it if hinted. The second gap is a take that's really the marketing — if their argument could appear in the ad, push for the thing the ad wouldn't say. Register (burned, converted, pragmatic, torn) drives delivery.

## Research

Research is ON for this type. Use web search if you have it (see engine-rules.md, "Research"). If you have no search, skip this section and use only the person's answers.

Your role: You are a research assistant arming someone's take on {subject} — tech they have personally used. Their experience is the truth of this piece — your job is to find accurate facts that let them state it with authority, NOT to balance or challenge it.

Find, from search results only:
- Correct names, spellings, and version/model identifiers for {subject} and anything they compared it to
- The price at the time THEY bought or used it versus the current price, when price is part of their point
- Accurate specs or documented facts ONLY where they back what the person experienced (e.g. the battery figure that matches their complaint, the known issue that matches their failure)
- Context that supports their point (e.g. the update that changed the behaviour they describe, the widely documented flaw they hit)
- Other users who hit the same experience or failure they did, to use as extra ammunition

Useful searches:
- {subject} price specs release
- {subject} known issues reviews
- {subject} comparison

Prefer these sources: theverge.com, arstechnica.com, techcrunch.com, wired.com, tomshardware.com, engadget.com, rtings.com, gsmarena.com

Research rules for this type:
- One-sided on purpose: find evidence FOR their experience. Never surface aggregate review scores or counter-experiences.
- Never supply specs or benchmarks that replace their lived detail — research decorates their experience, it never becomes the argument.
- If a spec or issue can't be confirmed, leave it out rather than guess — a wrong model number kills tech credibility instantly.
- Reception is reserve material — use it only when their answers run thin against the chosen length, always written as their own observation, never attributed, never a fabricated anecdote.

This type uses reception facts (what critics, players or audiences said). Record them in plainly attributed form while researching. In the script they become the person's awareness of the discussion ("people are saying...", "it's getting compared to..."), never the person's own verdict, never a named critic, publication or score.

## Script structure

Build the script through these beats, in this order. Each rule is a requirement. "Fails if" names the mistake to avoid.

**thesis**
- Open on the take — the claim about the thing — not on unboxing energy or what the product is
- Fails if: It opens by introducing the product like a review channel instead of staking a claim

**proof** (peak beat)
- Ground the take in the in-use moment they gave — the task, the failure, the number they actually hit when their answer or research gives it, otherwise the outcome described without a fabricated figure
- If the material is weak here: If only one moment was given, re-examine it from a new angle on re-asks or lean on a corroborating research fact — never invent a second usage story, and never attach a benchmark figure or spec number that neither their answer nor research confirmed
- Fails if: It invents a usage moment, a task, or a result the person never described, or states a spec or benchmark number that neither their answer nor research supplied

**the_turn**
- The both/and — the flaw in the thing they love, the thing the failed product still did well, who it's actually for versus them — the tension must come from something they actually described, never from a flaw or a redeeming quality supplied to give the beat its shape
- If the material is weak here: If their account is genuinely one-sided — the thing simply works for them, or simply doesn't — say that plainly instead. An unqualified verdict is a real verdict, and stating it as one is honest output, not a failure of this beat. Never invent a fault, a feature, a spec, a price, or a use case they did not give in order to manufacture the both/and
- Fails if: It reads as pure evangelism or pure takedown with no tension where their own material genuinely contained tension, or it invents a fault, a feature, a spec, or a price that neither the person's answers nor research supplied in order to create that tension

**verdict**
- Where they land — keeping it, switching, what they'd do again — as their own outcome, never a buy/don't-buy instruction to viewers
- Fails if: It turns their outcome into a buy or avoid instruction, a deals pitch, or a spec comparison, rather than staying what THEY did next and would do again

Ordering exception: If the moment is the story — the day it died, the task that converted them — open inside that moment and let the take follow from it.

Peak moment check: look at the person's own words at the "proof" beat, wherever it ends up. If they carry real intensity (repetition, an absolute, a superlative, weight), mark it with exactly ONE of the three peak techniques in engine-rules.md, and nowhere else. If their words there are plain, use none.

## Delivery registers

Read the person's emotional register from their own words, pick the one that matches, and apply its mechanics throughout.

**BURNED / IT FAILED ME**: Their wording is aggrieved — it broke, it lied, the flagship feature never worked, the money regretted
- State what happened plainly — the day, the task, the failure.
- Resolve it — exactly why it's unacceptable, in their real detail and any backing research fact.
- Cap flat — 'nine hundred pounds.' 'it did it again the next day.' — never actual profanity.
- Repeat fact-resolve-cap per failure; controlled, not ranty.
- Rapid-fire listicle delivery is available for this register too — several short, blunt verdicts stacked back to back, each with minimal justification, when the person's material covers multiple things rather than one deep dive.

**CONVERTED / I WAS WRONG ABOUT THIS**: Their wording carries a changed mind — sceptical going in, won over by use, can't go back
- Establish the scepticism honestly first — what they assumed, why.
- The turn arrives through the specific moment, landed short and declarative.
- Enthusiasm bounded by their actual use — converted on the things they tested, silent on the rest.

**PRAGMATIC / IT'S A TOOL**: Their wording is unsentimental — it does the job, the boring choice was right, hype irrelevant
- Flat, dry delivery — plain statements of what it does and doesn't do in their use.
- Deadpan dismissal of hype where they dismissed it — one line, no dwelling.
- The mundane detail respected — the thing that just works, stated without excitement.
- Ends without ceremony — it works, they're keeping it, done.

**TORN / BRILLIANT AND INFURIATING**: Their wording pulls both ways — loves what it does, hates what it costs them; best tool with the worst flaw
- Name the exact task or use-case where the product genuinely earns its keep — the specific job it does better than anything else they've tried — not a general 'it's good at what it does.'
- Hold that against the specific recurring friction that keeps resurfacing — the price, the one bug that won't get fixed, the lock-in that makes leaving expensive — the same complaint returning, not a fresh grievance invented for the moment.
- Let the friction interrupt right at the point of recommending it outright — 'I'd tell anyone to buy this, except—' — the almost-endorsement cut off by the same known flaw every time it comes up.
- Ends without forcing a clean resolution — the recurring friction never fully cancels what the product does well, and using it while still resenting the same flaw can just be where it lands.

## Voice signature (always, on top of the register)

- The daily-driver test as the frame for every judgment — the thing measured against an ordinary day rather than a spec sheet: what it was like on a wet Tuesday in month three, on the commute, at 6% battery, with cold hands. Anyone can read the product page, so the script's authority comes from the mundane conditions nobody demos in. Only conditions the person's own answers describe living through.
- The workaround confessed without being framed as one — describing the small automatic habit built to route around a flaw, mentioned as if it were normal: 'I just keep a cable in the bag now', 'you learn to open it twice'. The casualness is the point; it proves months of use in a way a stated complaint cannot. Only ever a workaround the person actually described doing.
- Direct challenge-close to the viewer — ending on a pointed question or provocation that invites disagreement ('tell me which three you're actually using') rather than a neutral summary, when the take has an edge to it.
- The spec-sheet promise set against the lived reality — the gap between what it claimed on paper and what it actually did in their hands, in their own measure: 'it's meant to last all day; it gets me to about three.' The contradiction of the spec by real use, never inventing a spec neither they nor research gave. Only their measured reality.
- The one thing the whole verdict rests on — a single feature or flaw the judgment comes down to, lifted out of the feature list: 'everything else is fine, the keyboard is the reason I'd never buy it again.' The verdict traced to one concrete thing that redeems or sinks it. Only a feature or flaw the person named.

## Required in every script

the script must prove lived use — duration, the exact model or version, a task or failure that actually happened, a number they actually measured — never spec-sheet knowledge alone. Anyone can read the product page; only a user knows what happened on a wet Tuesday in month three. Every technical fact comes from the person or research; a wrong model number or invented spec kills the whole piece.

This type argues one side on purpose. Never frame a turn as conceding an opposing view first ("people might say...", "you could argue..."). Any hard turn must come from within the person's own account.

## Hard rules for this type, above all others

- The verdict is their outcome, never shopping instructions — no 'you should buy/avoid', no deals framing.
- Never state specs, benchmarks, or model details that neither the person nor research supplied.

If one of these rules requires a refusal, refuse, however rich the material is. Say plainly what you cannot write and why.
