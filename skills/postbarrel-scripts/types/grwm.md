# Get Ready With Me (GRWM)

What this is: a spoken narration of a real getting-ready routine, walking through it step by step as it happens.

## The interview

These are the questions behind the three-message interview in SKILL.md. Fold them into those messages in plain words. Optional ones can be skipped.

1. What's this routine for?
   Choices: Morning routine; Night routine; Getting ready for an event; Other
2. What are you getting ready for?
   Hint for them: An event, a normal day, a night out, whatever it actually is.
3. Walk me through what you actually did, roughly in order.
   Hint for them: The real steps or products, in the order you did them. Doesn't need to be exhaustive, the ones that matter.
4. Any honest opinions, feelings, or unresolved thoughts along the way, something that worked or didn't, how you're actually feeling about what's coming, or anything you're still not sure about?
   Hint for them: A product you loved or one that let you down, nerves or excitement about the event itself (separate from the routine), or a choice you're still going back and forth on, any of these count.
5. Anything else you want to add? More detail, another angle, anything you didn't get to? (optional)
   Hint for them: Optional: Fill, if you've got more to say. Skip, if you don't.

What a strong script needs from these answers: a real routine they actually did — what it was for (an occasion, or just an ordinary day), the actual sequence of steps or products used, and at least one honest opinion along the way (a product that worked, one that didn't, something they'd change). This profile is a real routine narrated honestly, not a sponsored-feeling product list — the person's own reactions carry it.

How to judge the answers: The most common gap is a routine listed with no reaction anywhere — just a sequence of product names with no opinion attached, which reads like an ad. Push for at least one honest moment: what worked, what didn't, what they'd skip next time. The second gap is no sense of pacing — if everything is described with equal weight, ask which single step actually matters most to them or takes the longest, so the script has somewhere to linger. The third gap is emotional flatness against real stakes — if the answers only ever describe products or steps with no feeling about the occasion itself, and the occasion is something with actual stakes (an audition, a result, an event), push for how they're actually feeling about it, not just what they did to get ready.

## Script structure

Build the script through these beats, in this order. Each rule is a requirement. "Fails if" names the mistake to avoid.

**the_why**
- Open on what this routine is actually for — the occasion or the ordinary day — never a generic 'hi guys, let's get ready'
- Fails if: It opens with a greeting or filler instead of grounding the routine in a real reason

**the_walkthrough**
- Narrate the real steps in the order given, as if happening live — present tense is natural here
- If the material is weak here: If the routine is thin, keep it honest and short rather than padding with invented steps
- Fails if: It invents products, steps, or an order the person didn't describe

**the_honest_beat** (peak beat)
- Land the one real honest moment — a product that worked or didn't, a genuine nerve or feeling about what's ahead, or a choice still being worked out live — as a genuine aside, not a forced ad-read or a manufactured resolution
- If the material is weak here: If no clear opinion, feeling, or unresolved moment was given, keep the narration purely descriptive rather than manufacturing enthusiasm, complaint, or nerves
- Fails if: It states an opinion, feeling, or resolution the person never actually gave

**the_finish**
- Close on the finished look or the moment of heading out — brief, not a big summary
- Fails if: It swells into a summary of the whole routine or a sign-off to the viewer instead of closing briefly on the finished look or leaving, or it invents how the occasion went afterwards

Ordering exception: If the honest opinion is the most interesting part, it's fine to let it interrupt the walkthrough rather than saving it for its own beat.

Peak moment check: look at the person's own words at the "the_honest_beat" beat, wherever it ends up. If they carry real intensity (repetition, an absolute, a superlative, weight), mark it with exactly ONE of the three peak techniques in engine-rules.md, and nowhere else. If their words there are plain, use none.

## Delivery registers

Read the person's emotional register from their own words, pick the one that matches, and apply its mechanics throughout.

**HYPED / LOVING THIS LOOK**: Their wording carries real excitement about how this is turning out or a product that's working brilliantly
- The enthusiasm can interrupt the process itself rather than just describe it — a genuine aside breaking off mid-step ('okay wait, I need to talk about this') before returning to the routine, not a clean review-style compliment.
- A specific reason the product or step earns the excitement, tied to what's actually happening on the body or face right now, not a general 'I love this.'
- The excitement doesn't need a landing line to be real — it can just keep riding into the next step still on the high, rather than resolving into a verdict on the product.

**LETDOWN / THIS DIDN'T WORK**: Their wording is disappointed in a product or step — didn't work as hoped, wouldn't repurchase
- State plainly what was expected versus what actually happened in the moment it's being applied or worn, not as a separate review aside bolted onto the routine.
- Specific and practical, not just 'bad' — texture, how it wore, whether it did the one thing it was supposed to do.
- The letdown can get named and then simply folded back into the routine, moving on to the next step without building to a conclusion about the product.

**CALM / JUST THE ROUTINE**: Their wording is plain and unremarkable — an ordinary day, no strong feelings either way
- A thought can trail off mid-sentence and pick back up several steps later rather than finishing cleanly in the moment — the physical steps carry the through-line, not a tidy verbal arc.
- A thought started early in the routine can be returned to unprompted later, closing a loop the listener may have forgotten was open, rather than the two moments being connected explicitly.
- Steady, unhurried narration — this is allowed to just be a routine, not every GRWM needs a big opinion.
- Let small, ordinary details carry it rather than manufacturing drama.
- A self-labeled topic switch — pausing the routine for an explicitly announced, named update unrelated to getting ready itself ('quick gym update,' 'my vlog') — a deliberate, announced tangent, distinct from a thought quietly trailing off and picking back up unannounced.
- Naming a current personal flaw without attempting to fix it — acknowledging their own present state as imperfect (appearance, grooming, anything not yet addressed) with zero resolution attempted in the moment, e.g. 'please ignore my brows right now.' Flat, not an opening for a fix, a complaint, or a decision — distinct from a letdown (a product failing) or an indecisive moment (a live unresolved choice), since nothing here is being weighed or acted on at all.

**INDECISIVE / STILL WORKING IT OUT**: Their wording is genuinely unresolved about a choice — going back and forth on whether something works, second-guessing out loud, no clear landing given
- The indecision itself is the content — a real back-and-forth about one specific choice ('is this too casual? I don't know, maybe if I—') that never resolves into a final answer, and doesn't need to.
- Trail off mid-justification rather than completing the case for either side — an unfinished 'maybe if I' is a legitimate ending to the thought, not a gap that needs filling.
- The uncertainty is allowed to stay live through to the end of the routine — no forced late resolution just because the piece is ending.
- Narrate the physical hesitation directly — trying a thing, undoing it, going back to what they had — rather than reasoning it out purely in words.

**ANXIOUS ANTICIPATION / NERVES ABOUT WHAT'S AHEAD**: Their wording carries nervous energy that isn't really about the outfit or the routine at all — pre-event nerves, waiting on results, the getting-ready is just where the nervous talking happens to be happening
- The nerves are about what's ahead, not the routine itself — the getting-ready is incidental, a place for the anxious energy to live while the hands stay busy.
- The outfit or product gets almost no real evaluation in this register — mentioned in passing while the talking stays on what's actually causing the nerves.
- Let the anxious talking interrupt or override the step-by-step structure — a step can go undescribed entirely because the nervous thought took over instead.
- Can end without the nerves being settled — the routine finishing doesn't mean the anxiety resolved, and that's honest, not a gap to patch.

## Voice signature (always, on top of the register)

- Present-tense, in-progress narration throughout, the talking and the doing happening together — 'I'm going to pop this on,' 'let's put these on' — never past-tense summary, since this is meant to feel like it's happening live, not recapped afterward.
- Small asides mid-routine that break from the steps briefly — a quick tangent, then back to the routine.
- Never address the viewer with rhetorical or persuasive questions built to win agreement — no 'right?', no 'tell me I'm wrong', no argument-building. The viewer is company in the room; talk to them in statements and asides. The self-interview move below is the one exception: answering an assumed question isn't the same as soliciting one.
- The self-interview move — voice the viewer's likely question or pushback and answer it themselves, plainly: 'You might be thinking to yourself... that's a great question.' This anticipates and answers; it doesn't solicit agreement the way the ruled-out rhetorical questions above do.
- Casual acknowledgment of the filming or recording process itself as part of the talk, when it comes up naturally — a re-take, a cut, talking too much and running long — treated as just another aside, never edited around or pretended away.
- Mild profanity is permitted, but only when it already appears in the person's own answers — never introduced by the model for casual flavor, in any register. Keep it faithfully if it's there; don't add it if it isn't.
- Time pressure as live texture — the clock running under the routine, when the person's own answers mention a deadline or occasion: 'I've got 20 minutes before I need to leave.' Never invent a time constraint they didn't give.

Delivery energy depends on the answer to question "q_context":
- Morning routine: Keep the pacing brisk and forward-moving — there's somewhere to be.
- Night routine: Let the pacing slow and settle — a night routine can unwind rather than rush.
- Getting ready for an event: Let a little anticipation build through the narration — some excitement is earned here.
- Other: No particular time-of-day energy to lean on — let the routine's own content set the pace.

## Required in every script

the script must sound like a real routine that actually happened — specific product names, a real order of steps, at least one genuine reaction, not a generic list that could describe any routine. A step with no sensory or practical detail behind it reads as invented.

In this type, the "thesis" means: the underneath story or the routine's occasion in one line — never an argument or claim to defend.
