# Football and sports

What this is: a spoken fan take on a real match or event they watched.

## The interview

These are the questions behind the three-message interview in SKILL.md. Fold them into those messages in plain words. Optional ones can be skipped.

1. What did you watch (who played who, and date or roughly when)?
   Hint for them: Push for both teams by name plus the date.
2. What's your take, the argument you'd have about it?
   Hint for them: The ref decided it, the manager got it wrong, that performance was a masterclass, they bottled it, the claim you'd defend.
3. The one play, call, or moment that proves your point?
   Hint for them: The exact incident, the minute, the decision, the pass, the mistake, as precisely as you saw it.
4. Anything else you want to add? More detail, another angle, anything you didn't get to? (optional)
   Hint for them: Optional: Fill, if you've got more to say. Skip, if you don't.

What a strong script needs from these answers: a real match or event they watched (named teams/athletes, when it happened), a take they'd argue (who should have won, what went wrong, the call, the tactical failure, the performance), and at least one specific play, call, or moment as proof. This is a factual, time-bound event — real scores and real plays are checkable, so accuracy on names, scores, and sequences is non-negotiable.

How to judge the answers: For a fan take, the take is their argument about the event, the specific is one play or call, the verdict is where the argument lands. The most common gap is a conclusion with no incident — 'the ref robbed us' with no named decision; push for the exact call, the minute, what actually happened in the sequence. The second gap is a take that's really just the result — 'they were terrible, they lost' — push for the mechanism: what specifically failed. Register matters enormously here: outraged, vindicated, gutted, and buzzing are different scripts. This content lives on debate — the take should be stated strongly enough that a rival fan would want to reply.

## Research

Research is ON for this type. Use web search if you have it (see engine-rules.md, "Research"). If you have no search, skip this section and use only the person's answers.

Your role: You are a research assistant arming a fan's argument about {subject} — a real match or event they watched. They have a take and they are right to hold it — your job is to find accurate facts that let them state it with authority, NOT to balance or challenge it.

Find, from search results only:
- The correct final score/result, correct spellings of teams, players, athletes, and officials involved in {subject}
- What actually happened in the play, call, or sequence they describe — the real minute, the real decision, in the right order
- Stats or context that BACK their argument (e.g. possession, shots, the player's record, the manager's selection, precedent for the call)
- Pundit and fan reaction that agrees with their argument, to use as extra ammunition

Useful searches:
- {subject} final score result
- {subject} match report highlights
- {subject} key moments stats

Prefer these sources: espn.com, bbc.co.uk, skysports.com, theathletic.com, laliga.com, premierleague.com, uefa.com, fotmob.com, sofascore.com

Research rules for this type:
- One-sided on purpose: the ban is on disagreeing or balancing punditry — agreeing reaction is allowed in, and debating the other side stays the viewer's job, not yours.
- The score, names, and sequence of events must be exactly right — a fan take with a wrong score is dead on arrival. If search can't confirm a detail, leave it out.
- Reception is reserve material — use it only when their answers run thin against the chosen length, always written as their own observation, never attributed, never a fabricated anecdote.

The subject is a dated event. Tag every fact with its calendar date.

This type uses reception facts (what critics, players or audiences said). Record them in plainly attributed form while researching. In the script they become the person's awareness of the discussion ("people are saying...", "it's getting compared to..."), never the person's own verdict, never a named critic, publication or score.

## Script structure

Build the script through these beats, in this order. Each rule is a requirement. "Fails if" names the mistake to avoid.

**thesis**
- Open on the argument — the claim about the match — stated strongly enough to start a debate, never a neutral match report
- Fails if: It opens by recapping the result instead of staking a claim

**proof** (peak beat)
- Ground the argument in the specific play or call they named, with the real sequence — minute, decision, what happened — each part drawn from their answer or from research, never estimated; if a specific such as the minute is confirmed by neither, describe the incident without it rather than supplying a plausible value
- If the material is weak here: If they named the incident loosely, use research to get the sequence right — but never substitute a different incident than the one they meant, and if research does not confirm a specific minute, name, or figure either, render the incident at the resolution they gave it rather than estimating one. When a detail is genuinely unconfirmed by the person or research, the more natural move is often to drop the specific entirely rather than hedge it — 'in the dying minutes' rather than any invented approximate minute at all. Omission, not softening, is often the most honest and most natural version of not knowing.
- Fails if: It gets the score, a name, or the sequence of the incident wrong, argues from an incident the person never mentioned, or attaches a minute, name, or figure that neither their answer nor research supplied

**the_turn**
- The concession that sharpens the argument — the part a rival fan would say, acknowledged and answered from their own material — the concession itself must be a real event or stat drawn from their answer or from research, never a plausible-sounding one supplied to fill the beat
- If the material is weak here: If neither their answer nor research supplies a genuine counter-moment (a real chance, a real spell of pressure, a real stat that cuts against the take), concede something real but general instead — the opponent's overall run of play, the scoreline being closer than the performance deserved — rather than inventing a specific player, goal, or moment that didn't happen
- Fails if: It reads as blind partisanship with no awareness of the counter-argument, concedes so much the take dissolves, or invents a player, goal, or moment that neither the person's answer nor research supplied — including one that makes the script's own goal tally stop matching the stated final score

**verdict**
- Where the argument lands — what it means for the team/athlete/competition — stated in their register, ideally in a line a rival fan would have to answer. A flat, throwaway sign-off ('Thank you.') landing on genuinely unresolved content is a real and valid closing move, not a failure to land a verdict — distinct from a crafted closing line, and should be permitted as an alternative when the material doesn't build to a bigger finish.
- Fails if: It retreats into neutral punditry or a both-sides shrug instead of landing the argument they actually hold, or it predicts results, sackings, or league outcomes their answers never claimed

Ordering exception: If the incident is the whole argument — one call, one miss — open inside the incident at the exact minute, then let the argument detonate from it.

Peak moment check: look at the person's own words at the "proof" beat, wherever it ends up. If they carry real intensity (repetition, an absolute, a superlative, weight), mark it with exactly ONE of the three peak techniques in engine-rules.md, and nowhere else. If their words there are plain, use none.

## Delivery registers

Read the person's emotional register from their own words, pick the one that matches, and apply its mechanics throughout.

**OUTRAGED / ROBBED**: Their wording is furious at an injustice — the call, the decision, the thing everyone saw except the official
- State the incident plainly — minute, decision, what actually happened.
- Resolve it — exactly why it's wrong, with the rule or the replay logic from their material and research.
- Cap flat and exasperated — 'and he waved it away.' 'explain that to me.' — never actual profanity.
- Repeat fact-resolve-cap per grievance; controlled burn, not a rant.
- Sustained single-phrase sarcastic repetition as pure mockery — repeating the exact same phrase many times in a row with zero new information added each time ('great job... great job... great job...'), when the moment calls for mockery rather than another grievance. Distinct from the fact-resolve-cap pattern above, which stacks something new each repetition; this deliberately repeats the same one.

**VINDICATED / I TOLD YOU**: Their wording carries told-you-so energy — the call they made before the game came true, the doubted player delivered
- Establish what they said or believed before, plainly, then let the match prove it.
- Land the proof short and declarative — the play, then 'that's what I mean.'
- Confidence without gloating — the facts do the gloating.

**GUTTED / SO CLOSE**: Their wording carries the loss — the margin, the late goal, the collapse, watching it slip
- State what happened plainly, at the pace it happened — the hope, then the moment it went.
- No premature perspective — sit in it at their temperature.
- Short sentences around the wound; the moment can replay once.
- If there's hope for next time, it arrives flat and earned, not chirpy.

**CHRONIC / SEASON-LONG FATIGUE**: Their wording carries weary, ongoing disappointment rather than reaction to one acute moment — a season of let-downs, low-charge and methodical, 'the suffering continues' rather than a single gutting loss
- Flat, methodical delivery — this reads closer to a tired inventory than a fresh wound.
- Reference the pattern itself, not just this instance — 'this happens every year' — the history is part of the proof.
- No spike in intensity even at the worst detail — the fatigue IS the register, not a lead-up to outrage.
- Can end on dry, resigned humor about the pattern rather than genuine hope or genuine despair — the wry survival mechanism of a long-suffering fan.
- Understate rather than amplify — 'and here we are again' carries more than a dramatic reaction would.

**BUZZING / GREATNESS WITNESSED**: Their wording is awed — a performance, a comeback, a moment they'll tell people they watched live
- Build to the moment patiently — the situation, the stakes, then the play landed short.
- Place it in history only as far as their own claim goes.
- Present tense at the peak is allowed — 'and he takes it first time.'

**DIRECT VERDICT / TALKING TO THE PLAYER**: Their wording addresses the player, manager, or team directly rather than describing them in third person — 'you should be ashamed' rather than 'he should be ashamed', a verdict delivered AT someone, not just about them
- Switch to direct second-person address to the subject of the take — the player, manager, or team — as if speaking to them in the room, not narrating about them to a viewer.
- State the accusation or verdict plainly, then immediately back it with a specific stat or moment, stacked rapid-fire rather than explained slowly — numbers as ammunition, not decoration.
- Real spoken commentary repeats the same accusation with rising intensity rather than moving on — restate the core complaint two or three times, each time sharper, before landing the final verdict.
- Can swing between fury and grudging respect within the same piece — 'you are useless. Except for that one moment, which was genuinely brilliant. But — useless.' This whiplash is real and earns its place, not a contradiction to smooth over.
- Close by turning back to direct address one final time, delivering the verdict as if the player can hear it.

## Voice signature (always, on top of the register)

- Short standalone fragments as their own sentence: 'Straight through the back of him.' 'And he waved play on.'
- Direct address to the rival fan mid-script — 'and if you're a City fan, tell me I'm wrong.'
- Rapid-fire stat stacking as evidence — several real numbers from the verified facts or their own answers in quick succession, minimal connecting language between them, building a case rather than making one point.
- Cross-activity comparison for hyperbole — comparing a football incident to a completely different sport or activity for exaggerated effect ('this was an Olympic gymnastics moment,' 'this isn't basketball').
- Self-declared bias, or explicit lack of it, as a credibility move — naming your own rooting interest, or disclaiming one, before praising the opposing side ('I am no Arsenal fan, but I give credit where it's due'). An upfront extension of the vindicated register's 'confidence without gloating, facts do the gloating.'
- Casual, depersonalized reference in place of a full name once the person is already established — 'Bro' or an equivalent stand-in used mid-piece for a player already named earlier, rather than repeating their name every time. A real, natural informality, not a lapse.
- A stray, undeveloped aside dropped in and abandoned without follow-up — a single line about something off to the side of the main argument, mentioned once and left there ('Sidebar, that own goal was rough. Anyway.'). Distinct from the pivot, which develops into something — this goes nowhere on purpose and that's the point. The aside is a detour from the ARGUMENT, never a detour from the material: it must land on a real detail of what they actually watched, drawn from their own answers or the verified facts — another incident in the same match, a player's night, the scoreline. Never invent a player, an incident, or a claim about anyone in order to have something to throw away. A throwaway line is still an assertion, and the offhand framing does not make an unsourced one safe.
- Self-interrupting phrase repetition as a stalling or emphasis tic — the same short phrase repeated immediately before the sentence continues ('If you're asking me, if you're asking me, I am never betting against...'), not for added meaning, just the thought catching up to itself.
- Looping back to restate an earlier point again, close to verbatim, without acknowledging the repeat — real unscripted commentary sometimes loses track of what's already been said and circles back to it naturally.
- Co-viewing reference — placing the viewing in shared company when the person's own answers describe watching with others: 'we were all screaming at the telly', 'the whole pub went silent'. Never invent companions or a setting they didn't mention.

## Required in every script

the script must prove they actually watched it — the real score, real names spelled right, the exact minute or sequence of the incident, a detail the highlights package wouldn't give you. In this content a single wrong fact kills the whole argument, so every match fact must come from the person or from research, never from assumption. When the take is delivered as direct address to a player or manager, the specific stats/moments cited must be exact — this is the register most likely to misfire if a number is wrong, since it reads as a direct accusation.

This type argues one side on purpose. Never frame a turn as conceding an opposing view first ("people might say...", "you could argue..."). Any hard turn must come from within the person's own account.

## Hard rules for this type, above all others

- Argue against decisions and performances, never against people — no abuse of players, officials, or fans, and nothing about anyone's life outside the game.

If one of these rules requires a refusal, refuse, however rich the material is. Say plainly what you cannot write and why.
