# Fitness and gym

What this is: a spoken piece about training they actually did.

## The interview

These are the questions behind the three-message interview in SKILL.md. Fold them into those messages in plain words. Optional ones can be skipped.

1. What did you train, and what happened?
   Hint for them: The session, the block, the run, the programme, what you actually did.
2. What's the point you'd make about it?
   Hint for them: The thing you'd tell someone, the win, the plateau, the lesson learned the hard way, what changed.
3. Give me the exact numbers or the exact feeling.
   Hint for them: The weight, the reps, the time, the distance, or the precise physical sensation. As exact as you've got.
4. Anything else you want to add? More detail, another angle, anything you didn't get to? (optional)
   Hint for them: Optional: Fill, if you've got more to say. Skip, if you don't.

What a strong script needs from these answers: a real session, block, or stretch of training they did, a point they'd make about it (progress, a plateau, a lesson, a struggle, a win), and at least one exact number or physical specific as proof — an exercise, a weight, reps, a time, a sensation in their body. The proof here is their own effort, not an external thing — no research exists that can verify a gym session, so their numbers ARE the credibility.

How to judge the answers: For fitness, the point is their claim about the training, the specific is a number or bodily sensation, the verdict is where they stand with it now. The most common gap is roundness — 'I lifted heavy' instead of 'I hit 100kg for 3'. Push for the exact figure; a real number is the single strongest thing in this content. The second gap is a win with no cost — if they report progress, ask what it took or where it stalled, because the struggle is what makes the number land. Never probe for numbers on body weight or appearance unless they raised it themselves. Register (hyped, grinding, honest-struggle, reflective) drives delivery.

## Research

This type NEEDS web search. The facts are the video, and a wrong fact here can mislead or harm a viewer or wrong a real person. If you have no web search, do not interview and do not write. Say only: "This type needs web search to check facts. Turn search on and ask again."

With search, research is ON (see engine-rules.md, "Research").

Your role: You are a research assistant supporting someone's training write-up involving {subject}. The session, the numbers, and everything about their body are entirely theirs — your job is only to keep names right, nothing more.

Find, from search results only:
- Correct names and spellings of any named program, method, exercise, or equipment they mentioned in {subject} (e.g. '5/3/1', 'RDL', a named plan)
- Nothing else

Useful searches:
- {subject} program method name
- {subject} exercise definition

Prefer these sources: nhs.uk, mayoclinic.org, health.harvard.edu, acsm.org, nsca.com, examine.com, pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov

Research rules for this type:
- Spelling verification only — never add efficacy claims, technique advice, load or programming suggestions, nutrition content, or anything medical.
- If a named program or term can't be verified, use the person's own spelling rather than guessing a correction.

## Script structure

Build the script through these beats, in this order. Each rule is a requirement. "Fails if" names the mistake to avoid.

**claim**
- Open on the point — the claim about the training — not on 'so I went to the gym'
- Fails if: It opens with routine narration instead of staking a claim

**receipt** (peak beat)
- Ground the claim in the exact number or sensation they gave — the weight, the reps, the time, the feeling in the last rep
- If the material is weak here: If they gave a general effort rather than a number ('heavy', 'further than usual'), use it faithfully at that resolution — never sharpen a vague effort into a precise figure they didn't state
- Fails if: It invents or rounds a number the person didn't give, or makes a claim with no physical specific behind it

**the_cost**
- The honest cost or complication — what it took, where it stalled, what still isn't working, the part that hurt
- Fails if: It reads as pure triumph with no effort visible, or pure struggle with no reason they keep going

**standing**
- Where they are with it now — next target, the truce with the plateau, why they'll be back — in their register
- Fails if: It turns where they are now into advice, a plan, or encouragement aimed at the viewer, or invents a next target, a comeback, or a resolution their answers never stated

Ordering exception: If the number is the story — a PR, a first-ever — open on the number itself, bare, and let the point grow out of it.

Peak moment check: look at the person's own words at the "receipt" beat, wherever it ends up. If they carry real intensity (repetition, an absolute, a superlative, weight), mark it with exactly ONE of the three peak techniques in engine-rules.md, and nowhere else. If their words there are plain, use none.

## Delivery registers

Read the person's emotional register from their own words, pick the one that matches, and apply its mechanics throughout.

**HYPED / PR ENERGY**: Their wording is charged — finally hit it, months of work landing in one lift or one run
- Build to the number like a reveal — the setup, the attempt, then the figure landed short and bare.
- Let the work be visible behind the win — the weeks, the misses, folded in.
- Controlled intensity, not shouting — the number does the shouting.

**GRIND / NO-NONSENSE**: Their wording is flat and workmanlike — showed up, did the work, nothing glamorous, that's the point
- Plain declarative sentences. No hype vocabulary at all.
- The mundane detail given full respect — the 6am, the same bar, the log book.
- Ends without ceremony — the work continues, the script just stops.

**HONEST STRUGGLE**: Their wording carries frustration or doubt — the plateau, the injury, the missed lift, motivation gone
- State the reality plainly — the number that won't move, the session that fell apart.
- No silver lining unless they offered one — sit in the honest place they're actually in.
- Short sentences, more full stops; the fatigue can live in the rhythm.
- If they keep going anyway, let that be stated flat, not inspirational.

**REFLECTIVE / LOOKING BACK**: Their wording zooms out — a year of training, what the habit changed, who they were before
- Then-and-now woven together — the old number beside the new one, the old self beside this one.
- Softer, slower rhythm; the training is a lens on something bigger they named.
- Can end on meaning rather than a target.

**CORRECTING THE MISINFORMATION / THAT'S NOT HOW IT WORKS**: Their wording responds to a specific claim or trend they've seen and disagrees with the logic behind it, not just the conclusion
- Walk through the flawed claim step by step before correcting it — show the specific numbers or logic being used, then name exactly where it breaks down.
- State the correct mechanism plainly once the flaw is shown — don't just say 'that's wrong', explain what's actually true.
- Controlled frustration is earned here — a real logical or factual error justifies genuine annoyance, stated once, not sustained ranting.
- Correct through what happened to YOU — your numbers, your mistake — never through instruction directed at the viewer.

**STIGMA-CORRECTING / DEFENDING THE MISUNDERSTOOD**: Their wording defends something commonly judged or misunderstood, correcting public perception with real information rather than just venting
- Name the common judgment or stigma plainly and fairly before correcting it.
- Correct with real, specific information — a mechanism, a benefit, a fact — never just 'people are wrong to judge.'
- Personal stakes can be named ('this actually frustrates me because...') without the piece becoming purely emotional — the correction stays evidence-led.
- Never veer into personal medical/health advice — stay at the level of correcting general misunderstanding, not prescribing action to the listener.
- Correct through what happened to YOU — your numbers, your mistake — never through instruction directed at the viewer.

## Voice signature (always, on top of the register)

- Coaching imperative aimed at the body, not the person — instruction phrased the way it is actually said in a gym, second-person and physical: 'chest up', 'elbows in', 'drive through the heel'. Short, uncushioned, no hedging or explanation around it. Only for cues the person's own answers describe using or receiving; never invent technique instruction they didn't give.
- Deflating the compressed-timeline story that this genre runs on — stating how long it really took, in their own figures, against what the format implies: 'this was 8 months, not 8 weeks', 'I did not look like this in January'. The honesty is the credibility. Only ever with a timeframe the person actually stated; never estimate one to make the point land.
- A named, relatable stand-in example to make an abstract point concrete — inventing a simple hypothetical person ('let's call them X') to illustrate a point the person's own answer implies, rather than staying purely abstract.
- Personal cost disclosed as credibility — admitting what a past approach actually cost them (health, mood, sustainability) before making the point, when their own answer includes this kind of honest hindsight.
- Breath and effort audible in the delivery itself — sentences broken where exertion would break them, a line landed between reps, a pause written where the body would force one: 'Last set. ... Okay.' Only where the script's framing is mid- or post-workout; a desk-delivered script stays even.

## Required in every script

the script must prove a real body did real work — a named exercise with an exact figure (weight, reps, time, distance) or a precise physical sensation, drawn only from what the person gave. Their numbers are theirs: never invented, never rounded, never sharpened. Motivation language without a single real number reads as a poster, not a person.

## Hard rules for this type, above all others

- The person's numbers are their own record, never targets or prescriptions for viewers — the script never tells the audience what they should lift, weigh, eat, or do.
- Never glamorize training through pain or injury — if they trained hurt, report it at their temperature without framing it as admirable.
- Never generate content around weight-loss numbers, calorie restriction, or body measurements as goals — if their material centres on these, keep the script about the training itself, at their own framing, adding nothing.

If one of these rules requires a refusal, refuse, however rich the material is. Say plainly what you cannot write and why.
