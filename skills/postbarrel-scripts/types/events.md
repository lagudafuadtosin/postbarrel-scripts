# Events, concerts and festivals

What this is: a spoken piece about a live event they actually attended.

## The interview

These are the questions behind the three-message interview in SKILL.md. Fold them into those messages in plain words. Optional ones can be skipped.

1. What did you go to, and when, roughly what date?
   Hint for them: The act, event, or show, and where/when, as exact as you can.
2. What's your take, the one thing you'd argue about that night?
   Hint for them: Best show of your life, criminally short set, the opener stole it, not worth the ticket, say why.
3. The one moment from the night that stuck?
   Hint for them: A song, the lights dropping, a crowd moment, something said on stage, the second you'd relive.
4. Anything else you want to add? More detail, another angle, anything you didn't get to? (optional)
   Hint for them: Optional: Fill, if you've got more to say. Skip, if you don't.

What a strong script needs from these answers: a real event they were at (named act/event), a take (the one thing they'd argue about the night), and at least one specific in-the-room moment as proof — a song, a crowd moment, an exact point in the set where something happened. Being-there detail is the whole currency of this content.

How to judge the answers: For a live event, the take is their claim about the night, the specific is one in-the-room moment, the verdict is worth-it-or-not. The most common gap is setlist-summary — a list of songs played with no moment inside any of them; push for the one moment: which song, what happened in the room, what it felt like where they were standing. Ticket price and view from their spot are quiet gold if hinted. Register (euphoric, robbed, torn, nostalgic) drives delivery — a person describing the best night of their life and a person describing a 45-minute set for £90 need completely different scripts.

## Research

Research is ON for this type. Use web search if you have it (see engine-rules.md, "Research"). If you have no search, skip this section and use only the person's answers.

Your role: You are a research assistant arming someone's piece about {subject} — an event they attended. They were in the room and their experience is the truth of this piece — your job is to find accurate facts that let them state it with authority, NOT to balance or challenge it.

Find, from search results only:
- Correct spellings of the act(s), venue, and tour/event name for {subject}
- The specific date and leg of the tour they attended, confirmed before pulling any setlist or on-stage detail — on a multi-date tour, the wrong night's setlist is a credibility killer
- Setlist, running order, or event facts from that confirmed night that BACK what they described
- Context that supports their point (e.g. 'first show of the tour', 'the venue is known for X', 'the act said Y on stage that night')
- Other attendees' accounts of that same night that agree with theirs, to use as extra ammunition

Useful searches:
- {subject} setlist tour dates
- {subject} venue tour reviews

Prefer these sources: setlist.fm, songkick.com, bandsintown.com, nme.com, rollingstone.com, pitchfork.com, billboard.com, theguardian.com

Research rules for this type:
- One-sided on purpose: find facts FOR their experience. Contradicting attendee reviews are forbidden; agreeing accounts are the only outside voices allowed in.
- If a fact about that specific night can't be confirmed, leave it out — never guess a setlist or on-stage moment.
- Reception is reserve material — use it only when their answers run thin against the chosen length, always written as their own observation, never attributed, never a fabricated anecdote.

The subject is a dated event. Tag every fact with its calendar date.

This type uses reception facts (what critics, players or audiences said). Record them in plainly attributed form while researching. In the script they become the person's awareness of the discussion ("people are saying...", "it's getting compared to..."), never the person's own verdict, never a named critic, publication or score.

## Script structure

Build the script through these beats, in this order. Each rule is a requirement. "Fails if" names the mistake to avoid.

**thesis**
- Open on the take — the claim about the night — not on getting there or queueing
- Fails if: It opens with arrival logistics instead of staking a claim

**proof** (peak beat)
- Ground the take in the in-the-room moment they gave — the song, the drop, the crowd, the exact second when their answer or research gives it, otherwise the moment without a fabricated timestamp
- If the material is weak here: If only one moment was given, do not invent a second — re-examine the one moment from a new angle on re-asks, or lean on a corroborating research fact (setlist, set length); and if neither their answer nor a research fact fixes a specific time or count, render the moment at the resolution they gave rather than inventing one
- Fails if: It invents a song, on-stage moment, or crowd scene the person never described, or attaches a timestamp or count that neither their answer nor research supplied

**the_turn**
- The both/and — the transcendent hour inside the frustrating night, the price against the moment, what the room knew that the recording won't show — the tension must come from something they actually described, never from a reservation or a highlight supplied to give the beat its shape
- If the material is weak here: If their account is genuinely one-sided — the night was straightforwardly brilliant, or straightforwardly not worth it — say that plainly instead. An unqualified verdict is a real verdict, and stating it as one is honest output, not a failure of this beat. Never invent a complaint, a disappointment, a redeeming moment, or a detail of the night they did not give in order to manufacture the both/and
- Fails if: It reads as pure euphoria or pure complaint with no tension where their own material genuinely contained tension, or it invents a reservation, a highlight, or a detail of the event the person never described in order to create that tension

**verdict**
- Where they land — worth it, would they go again, what they'd tell someone with a ticket for the next night — in their register
- Fails if: It closes on generic gig praise ('what a night', 'go see them live') rather than their own settled verdict on THIS night, or it manufactures a would-go-again answer their answers never gave

Ordering exception: If the moment is the story — the lights dropping, one song — open inside it, mid-scene, and let the take about the night follow.

Peak moment check: look at the person's own words at the "proof" beat, wherever it ends up. If they carry real intensity (repetition, an absolute, a superlative, weight), mark it with exactly ONE of the three peak techniques in engine-rules.md, and nowhere else. If their words there are plain, use none.

## Delivery registers

Read the person's emotional register from their own words, pick the one that matches, and apply its mechanics throughout.

**EUPHORIC / BEST NIGHT**: Their wording is lit up — still buzzing, voice gone, the moment the room became one thing
- Build patiently to the peak moment, then land it short and declarative.
- Ideas can stack and tumble slightly — controlled momentum, not breathlessness.
- Present tense at the peak is allowed — 'and the lights drop, and the whole room just—'

**ROBBED / NOT WORTH IT**: Their wording is aggrieved — short set, bad sound, no-show energy, the price against what they got
- State what happened plainly — set length, price, what was skipped.
- Resolve it — exactly why it's an insult, in their real detail.
- Cap flat — 'ninety quid.' 'no encore. nothing.' — never actual profanity.
- Repeat fact-resolve-cap per grievance.

**TORN / BOTH**: Their wording pulls both ways — one perfect song in a flat set, loved the act and hated the venue
- Real back-and-forth, not tidy structure.
- Contradict a point a beat later.
- Stays genuinely unresolved.

**NOSTALGIC / IT MEANT SOMETHING**: Their wording carries history — waited years, a song tied to a person or a time, the act they grew up on
- Weave the past into the present moment — what the song used to mean beside what it meant in the room.
- Softer, more reflective rhythm; the night is a lens, not just an event.
- Can end on the meaning rather than the verdict.

**IN THE MOMENT / LIVE AS IT HAPPENED**: Their wording is present-tense, breathless, or recounting the night as it unfolded rather than reflecting on it afterward — the crush of the crowd, a moment they weren't sure would happen, genuine real-time reaction
- Present tense throughout, even though the telling happens after the fact — 'and the lights go down, and the whole room just—'
- Short, clipped reactions dropped in as their own beats — the exact feeling in the moment, not smoothed into narrative.
- Physical sensation given real weight — the crowd, the volume, the wait, the cold outside after — stated directly.
- Can end on the come-down rather than a big statement — the walk back, the ringing ears, the quiet after — not every night needs a grand closing line.

## Voice signature (always, on top of the register)

- Setlist position as the unit of time, not the clock — locating everything in the night by where it fell in the running order: 'third song in', 'by the time they played the one everyone came for', 'two songs before the end'. This is how someone who was actually standing there recalls a night. Only ever using songs, acts, or moments the person themselves named; never order a setlist they didn't describe.
- The crowd handled as a single body doing one thing — the room as a collective character rather than a backdrop: 'the whole place went', 'nobody sat down for the entire thing', 'you could hear the row behind us crying'. Only crowd behaviour the person actually described; never invent a reaction to raise the temperature of the night.
- The body's aftermath as proof of attendance — the physical receipt of having been there, stated flatly and usually as an aside: 'my voice was gone for two days', 'I couldn't hear properly until the Monday'. It evidences presence in a way no recording can. Only where the person's own answers mention it.
- The one moment the whole ticket came down to — a single instant singled out as the thing that made the night, or for a let-down, the flat moment that confirmed it wasn't: the song, the walk-on, the encore, the point they checked the time. The night reduced to its deciding second. Only a moment the person actually described.
- The wait carried as part of the event — the queue, the doors, the support act, the crush at the barrier, held as texture rather than skipped over: being there started before the first song. Only what the person's own answers mention of the lead-up.
- Sound and volume registered in the body, not the ears — the show as a physical event: the bass in the chest, the floor moving, feeling it before hearing it. The physical force as proof of the live-ness a recording can't hold. Only sensory detail the person actually gave.

## Required in every script

the script must prove the person was actually in the room — a named song at a specific point, where they stood, what the crowd did, the price, a moment no recording captures — never generic concert language alone. 'The energy was amazing' with no moment reads like someone who watched clips; one true room-detail reads like a witness.

This type argues one side on purpose. Never frame a turn as conceding an opposing view first ("people might say...", "you could argue..."). Any hard turn must come from within the person's own account.
