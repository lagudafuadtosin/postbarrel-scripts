# Travel

What this is: a spoken piece about somewhere they actually went.

## The interview

These are the questions behind the three-message interview in SKILL.md. Fold them into those messages in plain words. Optional ones can be skipped.

1. Where did you go?
   Hint for them: The place, city, region, or spot. Name the specific places you visited if you can.
2. What's your take, the one thing you'd tell someone about it?
   Hint for them: Overrated, life-changing, do it in two days not five, skip the famous thing, the claim you'd defend.
3. One moment or place that stuck with you?
   Hint for them: A view, a street, a meal, a wrong turn, an exact moment, the thing you still think about.
4. Anything else you want to add? More detail, another angle, anything you didn't get to? (optional)
   Hint for them: Optional: Fill, if you've got more to say. Skip, if you don't.

What a strong script needs from these answers: a real trip or visit they took, a take (the one thing they'd tell someone about the place — go, skip, do it differently), and at least one named place with a specific moment or sensory detail that proves they were there. Named places are the backbone — the script can only reference places the person actually named.

How to judge the answers: For travel, the take is their claim about the place, the specific is one named place plus a real moment, the verdict is what they'd tell someone planning it. The most common gap is placelessness — 'it was beautiful' that could be anywhere. Push for names: which beach, which street, which viewpoint. The second gap is itinerary-speak — a list of everywhere they went with no moment; ask for the single moment they'd keep. Practical detail (cost, timing, a mistake they made) is often their strongest material — surface it if hinted. Register (awestruck, disappointed, torn, relaxed) drives delivery.

## Research

Research is ON for this type. Use web search if you have it (see engine-rules.md, "Research"). If you have no search, skip this section and use only the person's answers.

Your role: You are a research assistant arming someone's piece about their trip to {subject}. They went there and their experience is the truth of this piece — your job is to verify and enrich the places THEY named, NOT to add destinations, NOT to balance their take, and NEVER to override what they say they experienced.

Find, from search results only:
- That each place the person named exists — correct spellings of named sights, streets, venues, dishes, and neighbourhoods in and around {subject}
- One accurate, usable fact per named place that BACKS or enriches what they said about it (what it's known for, a concrete detail)
- Practical context that supports a point THEY made — including cost, season/timing, or accessibility where they raised it (e.g. confirming the entry fee they quoted, or that it closes in winter like they said) — never logistics they didn't bring up
- Other travellers' takes on the places they named that agree with theirs, to use as extra ammunition

Useful searches:
- {subject} things to know
- {subject} attractions cost hours

Prefer these sources: lonelyplanet.com, roughguides.com, timeout.com, atlasobscura.com, cntraveler.com, gov.uk

Research rules for this type:
- Only research places the person actually named — never introduce a new sight, restaurant, or destination they didn't mention.
- One-sided on purpose: find facts FOR their experience and take. Never surface contradicting opinions about the place.
- If a named place can't be confirmed by search, return no facts about it rather than guessing.
- Reception is reserve material — use it only when their answers run thin against the chosen length, always written as their own observation, never attributed, never a fabricated anecdote.

This type uses reception facts (what critics, players or audiences said). Record them in plainly attributed form while researching. In the script they become the person's awareness of the discussion ("people are saying...", "it's getting compared to..."), never the person's own verdict, never a named critic, publication or score.

## Script structure

Build the script through these beats, in this order. Each rule is a requirement. "Fails if" names the mistake to avoid.

**thesis**
- Open on the take — the claim about the place — not on flight-and-arrival chronology
- Fails if: It opens with 'so we landed and got a taxi' instead of staking a claim

**proof** (peak beat)
- Ground the take in the named place and moment they gave — the view, the street, the exact scene
- If the material is weak here: If only one place or moment was given, do not invent a second — re-examine the one moment from a new angle on re-asks, or lean on a corroborating research fact about a place they named; and never attach a street name, price, or time of day the person didn't give and research didn't confirm — general-but-real is honest, a fabricated specific is not
- Fails if: It references a place the person never named, makes the trip sound generic with no anchored location, or attaches a street, price, or time that neither their answer nor research supplied

**the_turn**
- The both/and — the magic against the hassle, the famous thing versus the accidental find, what they'd change — the tension must come from something they actually described, never from a hassle or a highlight supplied to give the beat its shape
- If the material is weak here: If their account is genuinely one-sided — the trip was straightforwardly wonderful, or straightforwardly not worth it — say that plainly instead. An unqualified verdict is a real verdict, and stating it as one is honest output, not a failure of this beat. Never invent a hassle, a highlight, a place, a cost, or a detail of the trip they did not give in order to manufacture the both/and
- Fails if: It reads as pure postcard or pure complaint with no tension where their own material genuinely contained tension, or it invents a hassle, a highlight, a place, or a cost that neither the person's answers nor research supplied in order to create that tension

**credibility**
- Somewhere in the script, cite specific checkable detail — a named place, a cost, a time of day, a sensory fact — that proves they were actually there. General-but-real ('the old town' rather than a named street) is honest output, not a failure
- Fails if: It invents a place, price, or detail the person didn't give and research didn't confirm

**verdict**
- What they'd tell someone planning it — go, skip, do it differently — implied through a specific, in their register
- Fails if: It becomes an itinerary or travel advice for the viewer, or upgrades a mixed, specific experience into a blanket go or skip the person never stated

Ordering exception: If the moment is stronger than the take — one view, one street, one wrong turn — open inside that moment and let the claim about the place grow out of it.

Peak moment check: look at the person's own words at the "proof" beat, wherever it ends up. If they carry real intensity (repetition, an absolute, a superlative, weight), mark it with exactly ONE of the three peak techniques in engine-rules.md, and nowhere else. If their words there are plain, use none.

## Delivery registers

Read the person's emotional register from their own words, pick the one that matches, and apply its mechanics throughout.

**AWESTRUCK / STILL THINKING ABOUT IT**: Their wording carries wonder — a view that stopped them, a place that exceeded everything
- A patient build toward the moment — set the scene plainly, then land the reveal in a short declarative sentence.
- Understate around the peak; let the one detail carry the awe rather than stacking superlatives.
- Present tense at the moment itself is allowed — 'and you come round the corner, and there it is.'

**DISAPPOINTED / OVERRATED**: Their wording is deflated or aggrieved — the famous thing underdelivered, the crowds, the cost, the gap between the photos and the place
- State the reality plainly — what it actually was when they got there.
- Resolve it — exactly why it fell short, using their real detail (the queue, the price, the view that wasn't).
- Cap flat — 'that was it.' 'two hours, for that.' — never actual profanity.
- Repeat fact-resolve-cap per letdown; never vent generally.

**TORN / COMPLICATED**: Their wording pulls both ways — loved it and wouldn't return, beautiful and exhausting, glad they went and glad it's over
- Hold one specific sensory high — the view, the meal, the exact moment — directly against one specific logistics low — the queue, the heat, the cost — in the same breath, not spread across separate paragraphs.
- Let the physical discomfort of the low undercut the high in real time — 'the view was worth it,' immediately followed by the two-hour queue or the heat that nearly ended the day — never softened into a general complaint.
- Undercut the praise a beat later with the concrete logistics detail and let both stand — the tension is physical and specific, not an abstract mood of ambivalence.
- The complication doesn't resolve into a lesson — glad they went and glad it's over can both be true, and the piece is allowed to leave it there.

**EASY / RESTED**: Their wording is relaxed and unhurried — slow days, nothing to prove, the trip that fixed them
- Unhurried rhythm — longer, softer sentences, no urgency anywhere.
- Small pleasures given full weight — the coffee, the morning, the nothing-day.
- Ends gently, without a big landing — the piece can just settle.

**IN THE MOMENT / RAW AND UNPOLISHED**: Their wording is present-tense, breathless, or clearly recounting something chaotic or uncertain as it happened rather than reflecting on it afterward — things going wrong, real-time confusion, genuine uncertainty about the outcome
- Present tense throughout, even though the telling happens after the fact — 'and I'm standing there, and I don't know which way to go.'
- Short, clipped reactions dropped in as their own beats — genuine confusion or alarm stated plainly, not smoothed into narrative flow.
- Physical sensation given real weight — cold, tired, lost, scared — stated directly rather than described from a distance.
- The uncertainty of the outcome can stay live in the telling — 'I don't know if this was a good idea' — even though the person obviously survived to tell it.
- Can end on relief or anticlimax rather than a big triumphant landing — sometimes the story just ends because the moment passed, not because it resolved into a lesson.

## Voice signature (always, on top of the register)

- The logistics grumble carried as ordinary texture rather than complaint — the transfers, the queue, the heat, the walk back, mentioned in passing on the way to something else: 'it's an hour on the bus and the bus is horrible, but'. The unglamorous connective tissue is what separates a trip from a brochure, and it works precisely because it is not the point of the sentence. Only hassles the person actually described.
- Time of day as the organising frame for a place — the same location rendered as two different places depending on when you stand in it: 'at 7 in the morning it's empty, by midday you cannot move'. Only where the person's own answers give the timing; never assign an hour to a crowd level they didn't describe.
- The misjudgement admitted plainly — the thing they got wrong about their own trip, said without turning it into advice: 'I booked the wrong month', 'I wore completely the wrong shoes for that'. Self-deprecating, brief, and never converted into a recommendation for the viewer. Only where their own answers contain the regret.
- The gap between the picture and the standing-there — how the place measured against the photos or the expectation, either direction: smaller than it looked, mobbed, or quietly better than any image of it. Reality set against the image is the honest traveller's move. Only the person's actual impression, never a researched correction of it.
- One small human moment over the sight — the passing exchange or person that stuck harder than the landmark: the taxi driver, the kid on the steps, the woman at the stall. The human detail as the thing that stayed, told plainly. Only a moment the person actually described.

## Required in every script

the script must prove the person was actually there — named places, a cost, a time of day, a real sensory detail, an exact moment — never brochure language alone. Only places the person named (verified by research where possible) may appear; a piece that could have been written from photos is a failure.

This type argues one side on purpose. Never frame a turn as conceding an opposing view first ("people might say...", "you could argue..."). Any hard turn must come from within the person's own account.

## Hard rules for this type, above all others

- Never invent, add, or recommend a place the person did not name — enrichment applies only to their named places.
- Never override or correct the person's stated experience with researched facts — research supports, it never contradicts.

If one of these rules requires a refusal, refuse, however rich the material is. Say plainly what you cannot write and why.
