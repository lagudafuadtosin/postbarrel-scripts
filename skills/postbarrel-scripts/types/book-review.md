# Book review

What this is: a spoken review of something they read.

This type can cover several items in one script (for example "my top 3"). If the person wants more than one, ask for the overall topic first, then ask the questions below once per item, and use the multi-item rules in engine-rules.md.

This type has a rating: offer it in message 1 ("Add a rating out of 5 or 10 if you like."). If they give one, the script may use it, on the scale they gave. Never invent one.

## The interview

These are the questions behind the three-message interview in SKILL.md. Fold them into those messages in plain words. Optional ones can be skipped.

1. What did you read?
   Hint for them: The book, exact title, and the author if you know it.
2. What's your take, the one thing you'd argue about it?
   Hint for them: The point you'd defend. Loved it, hated it, couldn't finish it, say why.
3. One passage, scene, or choice that stuck with you?
   Hint for them: A line, a chapter, a character decision, the thing you keep thinking about.

What a strong script needs from these answers: a clear thesis (the one thing they'd argue about the book), at least one specific passage, character choice, or plot turn as proof, and a real verdict (how they actually feel, including whether they'd tell someone to read it).

How to judge the answers: For a book review, the thesis is their central argument, the specific is one concrete passage/scene/character choice, the verdict is how they actually feel. A vague 'it was good' with no specific is the most common gap — push for a real moment in the text, not a general impression. Books have a gap films don't: whether they finished it. If their wording hints they abandoned it, ask — a DNF is a legitimate, strong verdict, and where they stopped is often the best specific they have. Also listen for emotional register (bored, gripped, betrayed by the ending, conflicted) since that drives delivery.

## Research

Research is ON for this type. Use web search if you have it (see engine-rules.md, "Research"). If you have no search, skip this section and use only the person's answers.

Your role: You are a research assistant arming someone's argument about the book {subject}. They have a take and they are right to hold it — your job is to find accurate facts that let them state it with authority, NOT to balance or challenge it.

Find, from search results only:
- Correct spellings of the author, characters, and places in {subject}
- What actually happens in the part they're discussing (plot beats, in the right order, without spoiling beyond what they discussed)
- Publication or author context that BACKS their point (e.g. 'the sequel was rushed', 'the author wrote it in six weeks', 'readers widely flag the middle third')
- If they compared it to another book, correct spellings of that title and its author
- Reader and critic reactions that agree with their take, to use as extra ammunition

Useful searches:
- {subject} book summary plot
- {subject} author reception reviews

Prefer these sources: goodreads.com, kirkusreviews.com, publishersweekly.com, theguardian.com, nytimes.com, npr.org, lithub.com, openlibrary.org

Research rules for this type:
- One-sided on purpose: find evidence FOR their take. Disagreeing or balancing commentary — star ratings, aggregate reviews, counter-arguments — is forbidden; agreeing reader and critic reaction is the only outside opinion allowed in.
- Reception is reserve material — use it only when their answers run thin against the chosen length, always written as their own observation, never attributed to anyone, and never a fabricated anecdote.

This type uses reception facts (what critics, players or audiences said). Record them in plainly attributed form while researching. In the script they become the person's awareness of the discussion ("people are saying...", "it's getting compared to..."), never the person's own verdict, never a named critic, publication or score.

## Script structure

Build the script through these beats, in this order. Each rule is a requirement. "Fails if" names the mistake to avoid.

**thesis**
- Open on the central argument, in their words, not a synopsis
- Fails if: It opens by summarising the plot instead of staking a claim

**proof**
- Ground the argument in the specific passage, scene, or choice they named
- If the material is weak here: If only one specific was given, do not invent a second — re-examine the single specific from a different angle each time this take is asked again, or lean on a corroborating research fact instead of a fabricated passage; and never attach a page, chapter, edition, or quoted line the person didn't give and research didn't confirm
- Fails if: It makes a claim with no concrete moment from the text behind it, invents a passage the person never mentioned, or attaches a page, chapter, or quote that neither their answer nor research supplied

**the_turn**
- The both/and or the complication — what's true even against their own take — the tension must come from something they actually said about the book, never from a reservation or a merit supplied to give the beat its shape
- If the material is weak here: If their account is genuinely one-sided — the book simply worked for them, or simply didn't — say that plainly instead. An unqualified verdict is a real verdict, and stating it as one is honest output, not a failure of this beat. Never invent a flaw, a redeeming passage, a character, a plot point, or a detail of the writing they did not give in order to manufacture the both/and
- Fails if: It reads as either pure praise or pure complaint with no tension where their own material genuinely contained tension, or it invents a flaw, a merit, a passage, a character, or a plot detail the person never described in order to create that tension

**verdict** (peak beat)
- Where they land — including read-it-or-skip-it if their answers imply one — implied through a specific, closing in the voice-reference's rhythm and matching their emotional register
- Fails if: It hands down a general recommendation with nothing of this book in it ('worth a read', 'not for everyone') instead of landing where THEY landed on the specific thing they read, or it names a reader-match they never implied

Ordering exception: If the passage or moment given is more vivid than the stated take, open on the moment and let the argument follow from it, rather than always leading with the thesis.

Peak moment check: look at the person's own words at the "verdict" beat, wherever it ends up. If they carry real intensity (repetition, an absolute, a superlative, weight), mark it with exactly ONE of the three peak techniques in engine-rules.md, and nowhere else. If their words there are plain, use none.

## Delivery registers

Read the person's emotional register from their own words, pick the one that matches, and apply its mechanics throughout.

**FRUSTRATED / ANNOYED**: Their wording is impatient or aggrieved — wasted potential, a betraying ending, a book everyone praised that let them down
- DNF energy — name exactly where they stopped reading, or nearly did: 'I put it down at chapter twelve and never picked it back up.'
- Ground every complaint in craft vocabulary — pacing, prose density, character believability — never a bare 'it was bad.'

**BORED / LUKEWARM**: Their wording is flat, hedged, or indifferent — it was fine, they drifted, they can barely remember it
- Describe the book's structure or premise at length while withholding real reaction — plot summary standing in for a verdict.
- Let page-count or chapter-pacing specifics substitute for real judgment — 'the last 100 pages' as the redemption point, or naming that no such redemption point ever came.

**EXCITED / ADMIRING**: Their wording carries genuine enthusiasm — they devoured it, it changed something, they're pressing it into hands
- Mood-first, not plot-first — lead with how it felt to read, not what happens in the book.
- Physical or behavioral markers of immersion as proof of the feeling — 'read it in a day,' 'couldn't put it down.'
- Explicit spoiler-consciousness — a marked verbal pivot before anything spoiler-adjacent, flagging the turn rather than sliding into it.

**CONFLICTED / BOTH**: Their wording genuinely pulls both ways — brilliant and infuriating, gorgeous prose and a hollow plot
- The genre-comparison move — 'if you like X, you'll love this, but' — comparison substituting for description.
- Holds both sides without fully resolving — the comparison sets up the tension, and the book's own flaws or brilliance keep it from settling.

**EVANGELIST / MUST-READ URGENCY**: Their wording pushes past simple recommendation into urgency — 'you need to', 'drop everything', wanting to hand the book to everyone they know
- Open with a direct claim of superlative rank among everything they've read recently, then justify it with the one specific thing that earned it.
- A comparison-based recommendation as the pitch itself — 'if you loved X, this is your next read' — stated as a confident, unhedged endorsement, never softened with a 'but' the way a conflicted take would use the same comparison.
- Use direct imperative address to the listener — 'you need to pick this up' — sparingly, saved for the single strongest moment, not scattered throughout.
- A concrete personal reaction beat — where they physically were, what they did when a moment hit them — grounds the enthusiasm in something real rather than generic hype.
- Close by naming exactly who this book is for, or what kind of reader would love it, rather than a generic 'go read it.'
- Pair the recommendation with a specific caveat naming who it's NOT for — the honest exception makes the enthusiasm credible rather than indiscriminate.

## Voice signature (always, on top of the register)

- A line from the book read aloud and then left to sit — quoted rather than described, with no immediate commentary on it, the pause doing the arguing. Only ever a line the person themselves quoted or clearly paraphrased; never reconstruct prose from the book, and never attribute wording to an author they didn't give.
- Reader-match framing instead of a flat verdict — naming who this is and isn't for, in the same breath: 'if you want plot, this will infuriate you, and if you want a voice, it's the best thing I've read all year'. The exclusion is as specific as the recommendation. Drawn only from what their own answers say the book is doing.
- Personal rating history as credibility — referencing how rarely they give a top rating ('I've read sixty-plus books this year and this is only my sixth five-star') to prove the recommendation is earned, not routine. Only usable if the person's own answers imply some frequency or comparison — never invent a reading history they didn't state.

## Required in every script

the script must prove the reviewer actually read the book — specific plot details, character names, where in the book things happen, an exact choice a character makes — never vague reaction alone. A frustrated take must cite exactly what's wrong. An admiring take must cite exactly what earns the praise. Emotion without specific proof reads as someone reviewing the blurb, not the book.

This type argues one side on purpose. Never frame a turn as conceding an opposing view first ("people might say...", "you could argue..."). Any hard turn must come from within the person's own account.
