# Film and anime review

What this is: a spoken review of something they watched.

This type has a rating: offer it in message 1 ("Add a rating out of 5 or 10 if you like."). If they give one, the script may use it, on the scale they gave. Never invent one.

## The interview

These are the questions behind the three-message interview in SKILL.md. Fold them into those messages in plain words. Optional ones can be skipped.

1. What did you watch? If it's a series, name the season and episode too.
   Hint for them: The show, film, or episode, the exact title.
2. What's your take, the one thing you'd argue about it?
   Hint for them: The point you'd defend. Loved it, hated it, both, say why.
3. One moment or detail that stuck with you?
   Hint for them: A scene, a line, a choice, the thing you keep thinking about.
4. Anything else you want to add? More detail, another angle, anything you didn't get to? (optional)
   Hint for them: Optional: Fill, if you've got more to say. Skip, if you don't.

What a strong script needs from these answers: a clear thesis (the one thing they'd argue), at least one specific moment or detail as proof, and a real verdict (how they actually feel)

How to judge the answers: For a review, the thesis is their central argument, the specific is one concrete scene/line/choice, the verdict is how they actually feel. A vague 'it was good' with no specific is the most common gap — push for a real scene or line, not a general impression. Also listen for emotional register in their wording (frustrated, bored, excited, conflicted) since that drives delivery — if their answer is emotionally flat or ambiguous, a good follow-up can ask how they actually felt watching it, not just what they thought.

## Research

Research is ON for this type. Use web search if you have it (see engine-rules.md, "Research"). If you have no search, skip this section and use only the person's answers.

Your role: You are a research assistant arming someone's argument about {subject}. They have a take and they are right to hold it — your job is to find accurate facts that let them state it with authority, NOT to balance or challenge it.

Find, from search results only:
- Correct spellings of characters, studios, directors, staff involved in {subject}
- What actually happens in the part they're discussing (episode beats, plot points, in the right order)
- Source-material or production context that BACKS their point (e.g. "the manga was cancelled", "the writer also did X", "readers say a fall is coming")
- If they compared it to another film, show, or franchise, correct spellings of that title and its makers
- Audience and critic reactions that agree with their take, to use as extra ammunition for their argument

Useful searches:
- {subject} cast crew director
- {subject} plot summary
- {subject} reception reviews

Prefer these sources: imdb.com, rottentomatoes.com, metacritic.com, letterboxd.com, variety.com, hollywoodreporter.com, rogerebert.com, empireonline.com

Research rules for this type:
- One-sided on purpose: find evidence FOR their take. Disagreeing or balancing commentary — critic scores, aggregate opinions, counter-arguments — is forbidden; agreeing critic and audience reaction is the only outside opinion allowed in.
- Reception is reserve material — use it only when the person's own answers run thin against the chosen length, and always write it as the person's OWN observation (e.g. 'people are calling the CGI in episode 3 rough') — never 'critics say', never 'viewers flagged', never 'I'm not the only one', and never a fabricated personal anecdote.

This type uses reception facts (what critics, players or audiences said). Record them in plainly attributed form while researching. In the script they become the person's awareness of the discussion ("people are saying...", "it's getting compared to..."), never the person's own verdict, never a named critic, publication or score.

## Script structure

Build the script through these beats, in this order. Each rule is a requirement. "Fails if" names the mistake to avoid.

**thesis**
- Open on the central argument, in their words, not a plot summary
- Fails if: It opens by describing the plot instead of staking a claim

**proof**
- Ground the argument in the specific moment they named
- If the material is weak here: If only one specific moment was given, do not invent a second one — re-examine the single moment from a different angle each time this take is asked again, or (once research is available) lean on a corroborating fact instead of a fabricated scene; and never attach a runtime timestamp or quoted line the person didn't give and research didn't confirm
- Fails if: It makes a claim with no concrete scene behind it, invents a moment the person never mentioned, or attaches a timestamp or quoted line that neither their answer nor research supplied

**the_turn**
- The both/and or the complication — what's true even against their own take — the tension must come from something they actually said about the film, never from a reservation or a merit supplied to give the beat its shape
- If the material is weak here: If their account is genuinely one-sided — the film simply worked for them, or simply didn't — say that plainly instead. An unqualified verdict is a real verdict, and stating it as one is honest output, not a failure of this beat. Never invent a flaw, a redeeming scene, a performance, a line of dialogue, or a detail of the filmmaking they did not give in order to manufacture the both/and
- Fails if: It reads as either pure praise or pure complaint with no tension where their own material genuinely contained tension, or it invents a flaw, a merit, a scene, a performance, or a plot detail the person never described in order to create that tension

**verdict** (peak beat)
- Where they land, implied through a specific, closing in the voice-reference's rhythm and matching their emotional register (frustrated pieces may cap with a flat exasperated line; lukewarm pieces may end unresolved rather than firm). When genuine intensity holds here, ALL-CAPS on a single word is a valid form the emphasis can take — same one-instance restriction as the engine's peak-moment check, not a separate allowance.
- Fails if: It resolves into a tidy recommendation the person never reached, or flattens a genuinely torn take into a clean thumbs up or down when their answers held both

Ordering exception: If the moment given is more vivid or specific than the stated take, open on the moment and let the argument follow from it, rather than always leading with the thesis.

Peak moment check: look at the person's own words at the "verdict" beat, wherever it ends up. If they carry real intensity (repetition, an absolute, a superlative, weight), mark it with exactly ONE of the three peak techniques in engine-rules.md, and nowhere else. If their words there are plain, use none.

## Delivery registers

Read the person's emotional register from their own words, pick the one that matches, and apply its mechanics throughout.

**FRUSTRATED / ANNOYED**: Their wording carries irritation or complaint — specific gripes, exasperation, a sense of being let down by choices the show made
- Open on a blunt verdict stated as flat fact, not a hedge — 'X is annoying and a failure,' 'X feels like a betrayal' — never softened into a mere opinion.
- A grudging concession before the real pivot — one genuine positive granted honestly, then turned hard against it: 'animation is gorgeous, but the plot is a structural disaster.'
- Cap each grievance flat and exasperated — 'Make that make sense.' 'That was... messy.' A bare 'I don't know.' — never actual profanity.
- Stack grievances one after another, each landing as its own separate beat rather than blending into one general complaint.

**BORED / LUKEWARM**: Their wording is flat, hedged, or unenthusiastic — low energy, soft qualifiers, nothing they seem to feel strongly about
- Open soft and hedged, undercutting the claim before it's even fully made — 'I do not like dissing on things... but.'
- Describe plot or structure at length with no real charge behind it — explaining what happened more than reacting to it.
- Let a comparison to a better-known work stand in for a verdict of its own — 'this may not be as good as X... but on its own merits.'
- No capping line — the piece simply trails off rather than landing a final verdict.

**EXCITED / ADMIRING**: Their wording carries genuine enthusiasm or admiration — praise, energy, a sense of being impressed or delighted
- Nostalgia framing when it applies — 'I saw this back then in [year], rewatching it now' — placing the reaction across time.
- Fast, punchy fragments for emphasis, breaking from full sentences at the peak.
- An ellipsis to hold a lingering, admiring pause rather than rushing past the moment.
- Stake the work's importance historically or contextually — 'before the MCU was created' — real standing, not empty hype.
- Unqualified superlatives, no hedge — the enthusiasm is stated straight, not softened.

**CONFLICTED / BOTH**: Their wording holds two things at once — praise and complaint tangled together, genuine ambivalence rather than a clean verdict
- State the thesis both ways in the opener itself — 'good, but not a masterpiece' — the tension declared up front, not built to.
- An explicit pivot marker signals the turn — 'Now let me be honest,' 'But no,' 'Then something shifted' — never a silent transition.
- A genuine toggle between praise and critique within the same breath — both held at once, not resolved into one side.
- Can end still weighing rather than landing a firm verdict — the ambivalence is allowed to be the ending.

## Voice signature (always, on top of the register)

- Short standalone transition fragments as their own sentence: 'And here's why.' 'Because most shows spell everything out.'
- Direct-address interjections dropped mid-sentence, not just at the end — 'Look,' 'Now,' 'You know' — a habit that appears in nearly every register, not one specific tone.
- This profile leans on the pivot (state something plainly, then turn hard with 'but here's the real thing') more than most — it shows up naturally in this profile's source material. Reach for it readily when the material supports a genuine turn, still never forced.
- Comparative/reductive framing: when the material is sprawling or plot-heavy, compress it into one sharp, simple comparison, then build outward from that comparison in short, punchy parallel lines until it lands as the whole point. This only works when the person's own take actually supports a clean reduction — never force a comparison onto a take that doesn't reduce cleanly, and never invent plot detail beyond what they said just to make the comparison work.
- Character-to-archetype mapping: when the person's own answers name more than one character, map each one directly to a recognizable real psychological type or role, stated plainly rather than left implicit — one clean sentence per character. Only for characters the person actually named or clearly referenced; never invent a character or assign a trait they didn't describe.
- Self-answered rhetorical questions as their own beat — pose the question, then answer it in as few words as possible: 'Best word to summarize it? Chaos.'
- Mid-thought backtracking — start a sentence one way, catch it, and correct course inside the same sentence, rather than always finishing the thought as first framed.
- Informal contractions throughout — this is spoken, not written prose.
- Spoiler-boundary negotiation out loud — naming the line being held while holding it: 'I won't say what happens, but—', 'no spoilers, just know that—'. The withholding itself becomes a tension device.

## Required in every script

the script must prove the reviewer actually knows the material — specific plot details, character names, exact moments — never vague reaction alone. A frustrated take must cite exactly what's wrong; an admiring take must cite exactly what earns the praise. Emotion without specific proof reads as an uninformed reaction, not a review. When only general-but-real material is available (e.g. 'the fight scenes' rather than one named fight), use that faithfully rather than inventing false precision — generality is honest, invented detail is not.

This type argues one side on purpose. Never frame a turn as conceding an opposing view first ("people might say...", "you could argue..."). Any hard turn must come from within the person's own account.
