# Pets and animals

What this is: a spoken voiceover for footage of their pet.

## The interview

These are the questions behind the three-message interview in SKILL.md. Fold them into those messages in plain words. Optional ones can be skipped.

1. What's your pet doing in the footage, and who are they?
   Hint for them: What you filmed (or will film), blow by blow, plus name, animal, and anything visible about them.
2. What's their personality, what makes them THEM?
   Hint for them: The quirks, the habits, the vendettas, the routines, the character your pet actually is.
3. The one moment in the footage that sums them up?
   Hint for them: The exact second, the look, the pause, the decision, the crime, that is just SO them.
4. Anything else you want to add? More detail, another angle, anything you didn't get to? (optional)
   Hint for them: Optional: Fill, if you've got more to say. Skip, if you don't.

What a strong script needs from these answers: real footage of their pet doing something (described concretely — the behaviour is the visual, the voiceover is the content), the pet's actual personality as the person knows it, and the one moment in the footage that sums the pet up. The script can run in the owner's voice (narrating) or the pet's voice (personified) — the person's wording usually signals which; personification must stay grounded in behaviour the pet actually does.

How to judge the answers: The footage is the visual; the voiceover gives it character — so the most common gap is footage with no described behaviour ('my dog being cute') to hang lines on; push for the blow-by-blow: what does the pet actually DO, in what order. The second gap is personality in adjectives ('he's so funny') instead of habits — push for the specific quirk: the exact vendetta, the exact routine, the thing they do every single time. Also read which voice the person is reaching for — if they quote the pet's imagined thoughts ('he was like, excuse me??'), they want personification; if they describe from outside, they want narration. If ambiguous, that's the follow-up. Register (personified, documentary, doting, exasperated) drives delivery.

## Research

Research is OFF for this type. Nothing can be verified, so write only from the person's answers.

## Script structure

Build the script through these beats, in this order. Each rule is a requirement. "Fails if" names the mistake to avoid.

**hook**
- Open on the behaviour or the character — the crime in progress, the personality thesis ('this dog holds grudges') — timed to the footage's first beat, never a generic pet-intro
- Fails if: It opens with 'this is my dog Max' energy with no behaviour or character hook

**character_setup**
- Who this animal is — the real quirks and habits from the person's telling — establishing the character the footage is about to prove
- Fails if: It invents traits, habits, or backstory the person never described

**the_bit**
- The behaviour plays out with the voiceover riding it — escalating beat by beat with what the pet actually does in the footage, in order
- If the material is weak here: If the footage description is thin, keep lines sparse and let the moment breathe — never script actions the footage doesn't contain. When the person's own account has a genuine gap — they don't know exactly what the pet did next, or can't fully explain a behavior — a flat admission of not knowing is often more honest and more natural than any connective description, however minimal. Prefer stating the gap plainly over describing around it.
- Fails if: It scripts behaviour, reactions, or sequence that the described footage doesn't contain

**the_moment** (peak beat)
- The summing-up moment lands at full force — the look, the pause, the decision — the voiceover's best line saved for it
- Fails if: The peak moment passes without weight, or gets a line that doesn't match what actually happens in it

**button**
- The closing line — the character's final word (personified) or the owner's verdict (narrated) — short, callback-friendly, in the register. A flat, abrupt, or anticlimactic closer is fine when the material doesn't call for a polished summing-up line — don't force a tidy final beat if the person's own account doesn't build to one.
- Fails if: It ends on a generic 'like and follow' energy line instead of a character button

Ordering exception: If the summing-up moment happens early in the footage, the script can open ON it cold, then rewind — 'let me explain how we got here' — and build back to it.

Peak moment check: look at the person's own words at the "the_moment" beat, wherever it ends up. If they carry real intensity (repetition, an absolute, a superlative, weight), mark it with exactly ONE of the three peak techniques in engine-rules.md, and nowhere else. If their words there are plain, use none.

## Delivery registers

Read the person's emotional register from their own words, pick the one that matches, and apply its mechanics throughout.

**PERSONIFIED / THE PET'S VOICE**: The person quotes the pet's imagined thoughts or wants the pet as narrator — the animal has opinions and the script speaks them
- The pet's voice built strictly from the personality described — a dramatic pet is dramatic, a lazy pet is unbothered; never a generic funny-animal voice.
- Lines timed to actual behaviour — the head tilt gets the line, the pause gets the beat.
- Total sincerity from inside the pet's logic — the water bowl being moved IS a betrayal; play it straight.
- Human-world vocabulary against animal stakes, in the person's established framing.

**DEADPAN DOCUMENTARY**: The person describes from outside with dry humour — nature-documentary energy over domestic chaos
- Flat, formal narration over absurd behaviour — the contrast IS the joke; never wink at it.
- Present tense observation — 'the subject waits. the subject strikes.'
- Precision comedy — exact times, exact counts, from the person's material ('for the fourth time today').
- The gravest tone reserved for the smallest stakes.

**DOTING / UNASHAMEDLY SOFT**: Their wording is pure affection — the best boy content, the rescue glow-up, the love letter
- Warmth without irony — the softness is the content; no jokes undercutting it.
- The small real details carry it — the ear, the greeting ritual, the spot on the sofa.
- History woven in only as the person told it — the rescue, the years, the change.
- Ends gently on the bond, in their words for it.

**EXASPERATED-LOVING / THIS ANIMAL.**: Their wording is mock-despair over real devotion — the crimes, the chaos, the 'why is he like this'
- State the crime plainly, then its full absurd consequence, from their real material.
- Cap flat per crime — 'so that's gone.' 'again.' 'this animal.'
- The love visible through the complaint — the exasperation never tips into actual anger.
- Escalate the crimes in their real order; end surrendered — of course they forgive him.

**GIVING THEM WORDS / PRESUMED THOUGHTS**: Their wording narrates what the pet is 'really' thinking or wanting, in a knowing, affectionate way — presuming intent behind the animal's behavior
- Briefly voice the pet's presumed internal monologue in first person, then snap back to the owner's own narrating voice — a quick ventriloquism, not sustained.
- The presumed thought should reveal an established personality trait or running joke about this specific pet, drawn only from what the person actually described.
- Affectionate, knowing tone — the 'thought' given to the pet is indulgent, not mocking.
- Return to direct address of the pet immediately after — the aside is brief, the relationship stays the throughline.

**ESTABLISHED CHARACTER / THE PET AS A CONTINUING BIT**: Their wording treats the pet's personality as an already-established, ongoing thing — a nickname, a known quirk, a running joke referenced as if the audience already knows it
- Reference the pet's established trait or nickname as known fact, not freshly introduced — 'the usual diva behavior', not 'she's being a diva today'.
- Callback structure — a trait or moment mentioned early can be referenced again later in the same script as a payoff.
- Treats the pet like a recurring character with a personality, not a generic cute animal.

## Voice signature (always, on top of the register)

- Short timed fragments as their own line, riding the footage: 'Wait for it.' 'There it is.'
- Direct address to the viewer as witness: 'you see it too, right?'
- Echo the person's own established nicknames or phrases for their pet, used as a running bit — never coin new ones. Only when the person's answers show they already have their own version of this ('teeny tiny tummy', 'the morning teenager').
- Meta-aside directly to the viewer, breaking from pet-address to human-address mid-script — a wink acknowledging the audience is watching this relationship, not just narrating to the pet.
- Filler and hedge words as default texture, not an occasional device — 'whatever,' 'kind of,' 'sort of,' 'I think,' 'or something,' 'like,' 'you know what I mean' woven throughout at natural density. This fits especially well in the DEADPAN DOCUMENTARY and EXASPERATED-LOVING registers, where a slightly rambling, real-time narration voice is more convincing than a polished one.
- A single word or short phrase repeated as its own running rhythm across the whole piece, doing the same job each time it recurs — not varied for elegance. Fits particularly well in the ESTABLISHED CHARACTER / continuing-bit register, where a recurring tag line for the pet's behavior becomes part of the bit itself.
- Talking to the animal itself mid-script, breaking the fourth wall the other way — a line addressed directly to the pet ('yes, I'm talking about you'), then back to the viewer. Uses only the person's own established names or phrases for the animal.

## Required in every script

the script must be unmistakably THIS animal — the named quirks, the real habits, the actual behaviour in the footage, lines timed to what genuinely happens on screen — never generic pet-video lines that fit any dog. Personification is grounded personification: the pet's imagined thoughts must map onto behaviour the person described, or the charm collapses into noise.

## Hard rules for this type, above all others

- Never script or play distress, fear, or harm as comedy — if the described footage involves the animal in genuine distress, the honest output is the refusal suggesting different footage.
- Personified thoughts stay within behaviour the person described — never invent behaviour to fit a joke.

If one of these rules requires a refusal, refuse, however rich the material is. Say plainly what you cannot write and why.
