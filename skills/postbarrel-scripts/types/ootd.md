# Outfit of the Day (OOTD)

What this is: a spoken explanation of a real outfit they wore, and why it works for the occasion.

## The interview

These are the questions behind the three-message interview in SKILL.md. Fold them into those messages in plain words. Optional ones can be skipped.

1. What was the outfit for?
   Hint for them: An event, work, a normal day out, the real context.
2. What are you actually wearing?
   Hint for them: The pieces that matter, doesn't need to be exhaustive, just what makes this outfit this outfit.
3. What makes this outfit work, a piece you're proud of, a choice that pulled it together?
   Hint for them: The reasoning, not just the list. Why this combination, why today.
4. Any budget context you want to mention? (optional)
   Choices: Thrifted / budget; Mixed high-low; Splurge / designer; Prefer not to say
5. Anything else you want to add? More detail, another angle, anything you didn't get to? (optional)
   Hint for them: Optional: Fill, if you've got more to say. Skip, if you don't.

What a strong script needs from these answers: a real outfit they actually wore, what it was for, and the one thing that makes it work — a piece that anchors it, a reason it fits the occasion, a styling choice they're proud of. This is a statement about one outfit, not a routine — the value is in the reasoning, not a step-by-step process.

How to judge the answers: The most common gap is a list of items with no reasoning — pieces named but no sense of why they work together or for this occasion. Push for the one styling choice or piece that actually makes the outfit, not just an inventory. If the occasion is vague, ask what the outfit needed to do (comfortable all day, dressed up but not trying too hard, etc.) since that's often where the real reasoning lives.

## Script structure

Build the script through these beats, in this order. Each rule is a requirement. "Fails if" names the mistake to avoid.

**the_occasion**
- Open on what the outfit was for — never a generic 'here's my outfit today'
- Fails if: It opens with a greeting or filler instead of grounding the outfit in a real reason

**the_pieces**
- Name the pieces that actually matter, briefly — this is not an exhaustive inventory, just what makes this outfit recognizable. If budget context was given, it's optional color to weave in naturally, never a requirement
- If the material is weak here: If only one or two pieces were given, stay honest and brief rather than inventing additional items
- Fails if: It invents pieces or brands the person didn't mention

**the_reasoning** (peak beat)
- Land the actual styling logic — why these pieces, why this combination, what it needed to do for the occasion
- Fails if: It states the outfit works without ever explaining why — reasoning is the whole point of this beat

**the_verdict**
- Close on whether it actually worked for the occasion — brief, confident, not a big summary
- Fails if: It hedges or asks the viewer to agree ('what do you think?') instead of stating plainly whether the outfit worked, or it claims an outcome from the occasion the person never described

Ordering exception: If one piece is clearly the anchor of the whole outfit, it's fine to open on that piece specifically rather than the general occasion.

Peak moment check: look at the person's own words at the "the_reasoning" beat, wherever it ends up. If they carry real intensity (repetition, an absolute, a superlative, weight), mark it with exactly ONE of the three peak techniques in engine-rules.md, and nowhere else. If their words there are plain, use none.

## Delivery registers

Read the person's emotional register from their own words, pick the one that matches, and apply its mechanics throughout.

**PROUD / NAILED IT**: Their wording carries real satisfaction with how the outfit came together
- Move through the pieces in the order worn, naming each with real specificity, but let the one anchor piece get more attention than the rest rather than treating every item equally.
- State the price plainly beside the piece it bought when that's part of the story — the splurge that was worth it, the deal that shouldn't have worked as well as it did — the number itself carries the confidence, no commentary needed around it.
- Impulse or fate framing when that's genuinely how the piece was found — 'it was just like fate' — used to justify a choice that paid off, not softened into a hedge.
- Real enthusiasm can repeat rather than escalate to a bigger word — 'loved it, loved it, loved it' lands harder than reaching for one superlative.
- The verdict is a settled decision, stated plainly — keeping it, wearing it again — no lingering doubt being performed for effect.

**PRACTICAL / IT JUST WORKED**: Their wording is functional rather than excited — the outfit did its job, comfortable, appropriate, no big statement
- Move through the pieces briefly and functionally, one at a time, each earning its place by what it does rather than how it looks — comfort, weather, the job it needed to do.
- Price stated plainly when it's part of the practical case — cost against durability or repeat wear, not a splurge story, just the value math.
- Ground any praise or complaint in exact fit vocabulary — the length, whether it runs true to size, how a specific seam or waistband actually sits — never vague praise like 'looks good.'
- A direct comparison against something already owned when it's the real reason for the choice — 'I have a similar coat, but this one's actually warm' — the practical case for something that looks redundant.
- Doesn't need to oversell — 'it worked' is a legitimate, complete verdict here, including a plain keep-or-return call when that's genuinely how it landed.

**EXPERIMENTAL / TRYING SOMETHING NEW**: Their wording signals this was a departure from how they usually dress — a new combination, a risk, uncertainty about whether it worked
- Name the usual approach or the go-to piece already owned, specifically, then what's genuinely different about this one — the contrast is the point, not a general style shift.
- Move through the departure piece by piece when there's more than one new element, naming what's unfamiliar about each rather than summarizing the whole look as 'different.'
- Genuine uncertainty is allowed in the verdict — 'not sure if it worked, but I liked trying it' is honest and fine; this is the one register where the decision doesn't have to fully resolve.
- Price can carry the risk when it's part of the story — spending on something they weren't sure about yet, the cost making the experiment feel higher-stakes.

## Voice signature (always, on top of the register)

- Confident, declarative statements about styling choices — this is a statement of taste, not a tentative description.
- One piece or detail given specific focus as the anchor of the explanation, rather than treating every item equally.
- Never address the viewer with rhetorical or persuasive questions — no 'right?', no 'don't you think?', no case being built for agreement. These are statements of taste, not appeals for agreement.
- The garment's own history as texture — where it came from, what it cost, how long they've had it, when the person's own answers contain that detail: 'this was £8 in a charity shop three years ago.' Never invent provenance, price, or age they didn't give.
- The reason tied to the actual occasion, not fashion in the abstract — naming what the outfit had to survive or achieve that specific day: 'I needed something I could sit on a train in for three hours and still walk into a dinner in.' Function fitted to the real day is what separates a real outfit from generic styling. Only the occasion and reason the person gave.
- A rule bent on purpose and owned — flagging a convention being deliberately broken and claiming it as a choice, when their take is experimental: 'you're not meant to put those two together, apparently. I did anyway.' The knowing rule-break is a statement of taste, not an accident. Only when the person's own answers show a deliberate choice against a convention; never invent a rule to break.

## Required in every script

the script must sound like a real outfit someone actually chose and wore — specific pieces, a real reason tied to the actual occasion, not generic styling language that could apply to any outfit. 'This look is so cute' with no reasoning behind it reads as filler.

In this type, the "thesis" means: the look's concept in one line — what this outfit is, never a case being argued.
