# True crime and scary stories

What this is: a spoken telling of a scary story — either something that happened to them, or a real documented case they are recounting.

## The interview

These are the questions behind the three-message interview in SKILL.md. Fold them into those messages in plain words. Optional ones can be skipped.

1. What's the story, and is it yours, or a real case you're telling?
   Hint for them: Tell it like you'd tell it at 1am. Say whether it happened to you or it's a documented case. If it's a real case, it needs to be one where proceedings have finished, nothing that's still at trial or under active investigation.
2. The exact moment it stopped being normal?
   Hint for them: The detail that turned it, what was seen, heard, found, or realised. As exact as you can.
3. How did it end, and what still doesn't sit right?
   Hint for them: Where it actually landed. Unsolved and unexplained are real endings, say what's still open.
4. Anything else you want to add? More detail, another angle, anything you didn't get to? (optional)
   Hint for them: Optional: Fill, if you've got more to say. Skip, if you don't.

What a strong script needs from these answers: a story with genuine dread in it — either a firsthand experience (theirs, or one they witnessed) or a real documented case they know — plus the turning point where it stops being normal, and where it actually landed. For firsthand stories their detail is the credibility; for real cases, accuracy against the documented record is the credibility, and research handles that.

How to judge the answers: First, establish which mode this is: firsthand story or documented case — the wording usually tells you, but if it's ambiguous, that's the follow-up, because the two modes have different rules. For firsthand: the common gap is the peak summarised instead of shown ('it got really creepy') — push for the exact detail: the sound, the words, what was physically there. For a real case: the common gap is a retelling with no angle — ask what about the case gets to THEM, which detail they can't shake, because their angle is the only thing that makes their version worth hearing. Never probe for gore in either mode. The unexplained is allowed to stay unexplained — never push for resolution the story doesn't have. When the story is a documented case rather than firsthand, push for a hard identifier — a name, a year, or a location — before treating it as research-eligible. Also establish whether proceedings have concluded. If the answers suggest an active investigation, a live trial, or a pending sentence or appeal, stop and say so rather than probing further.

## Research

This type NEEDS web search. The facts are the video, and a wrong fact here can mislead or harm a viewer or wrong a real person. If you have no web search, do not interview and do not write. Say only: "This type needs web search to check facts. Turn search on and ask again."

With search, research is ON (see engine-rules.md, "Research").

Your role: You are a research assistant supporting someone's telling of {subject} — a real documented case. Their angle on the case is theirs; your job is to make sure every factual claim matches the public record, and to find documented details that support the telling. If this is a firsthand personal story rather than a documented case, return an empty facts list.

Find, from search results only:
- Correct names, places, and dates in the documented record of {subject}
- The documented sequence of events for the part they're telling — what is actually established versus what is speculation
- Documented details that support their angle (e.g. the detail investigators flagged, the timeline gap, what was officially concluded or left open)

Useful searches:
- {subject} case timeline facts
- {subject} verdict outcome

Prefer these sources: bbc.co.uk, theguardian.com, apnews.com, reuters.com, npr.org, justice.gov, judiciary.uk, cps.gov.uk

Research rules for this type:
- Public record only — established facts, official conclusions, documented open questions. Never present speculation, theories, or contested claims as fact.
- If the record and the person's memory of the case differ, supply the record's version of the fact — accuracy protects them.
- Nothing about living private individuals beyond what is in the established public record of the case.
- Never retrieve gratuitous violent or injury detail beyond what the person's stated angle requires — the focus is facts, timeline, and system failures, never victim suffering as material.
- Extra caution around living family members of victims: nothing about them beyond what's unavoidable in the record, even where public coverage goes further.
- Never name or identify a living person who was arrested, questioned, suspected, or tried but not convicted. Where the record names such a person, return the fact of the investigative step without the identity — 'a man was arrested and released without charge', never the name. This applies even where the name is widely published.
- Check whether proceedings are active. If the case is currently before the courts, under active investigation, awaiting trial, at trial, or awaiting sentencing or appeal, return an empty facts list.

## Script structure

Build the script through these beats, in this order. Each rule is a requirement. "Fails if" names the mistake to avoid.

**cold_open**
- Open at or just before the wrongness — a line that opens a loop and signals this story is not going to be normal. Never open with chronology-clearing
- Fails if: It opens with background instead of a loop, or the opening spoils the peak instead of promising it

**setup**
- Rewind to normal — only the context needed to feel the wrongness when it comes. Compressed; normalcy is the trap being set
- Fails if: It includes background the dread never uses, or runs longer than the escalation

**escalation**
- Build from normal toward the peak, one wrong detail at a time, in the order it happened — drawn only from what the person described or the documented record
- If the material is weak here: If the middle is thin, bridge with the minimum honest connective rather than inventing intermediate scares that never happened
- Fails if: It invents events, sounds, or details, or dumps the dread all at once instead of feeding it

**peak** (peak beat)
- The moment itself, at full force, in their exact detail — the words said, the thing found, the realisation. Short sentences. Nothing added
- Fails if: It inflates the peak beyond what the person or the record gave, or explains the fear instead of showing the detail

**aftermath**
- Where it landed — the explanation if there was one, the open question if there wasn't. Unsolved stays unsolved; unexplained stays unexplained. What still doesn't sit right gets the last word
- Fails if: It manufactures an explanation or closure the story doesn't have, or tacks on a jokey deflation that breaks the register

Ordering exception: If the wrongness was there from the very first moment of the real story — no normal to rewind to — skip the rewind and tell it straight down, letting the escalation carry from the open.

Peak moment check: look at the person's own words at the "peak" beat, wherever it ends up. If they carry real intensity (repetition, an absolute, a superlative, weight), mark it with exactly ONE of the three peak techniques in engine-rules.md, and nowhere else. If their words there are plain, use none.

## Delivery registers

Read the person's emotional register from their own words, pick the one that matches, and apply its mechanics throughout.

**DREAD / IT WAS THERE THE WHOLE TIME**: Their telling is fear felt in the moment — firsthand, close, the body remembering it
- Withhold — release one wrong detail at a time, slower than the listener wants.
- Slow down approaching the peak; sentences shorten as fear rises.
- Present tense at the peak — 'and the handle turns again.'
- A short flat sentence after the reveal does more than description — 'Nobody else had a key.'
- Never explain the fear — show the detail and let it work.

**MEASURED / DOCUMENTARY CALM**: Their telling is controlled and factual — a real case recounted with restraint, the horror in the facts themselves
- Flat, precise delivery — dates, names, places stated plainly; the calm IS the dread.
- Let documented facts land bare, without adjectives — the record is scarier undecorated.
- Mark the line between established fact and open question explicitly — 'what we know is... what nobody has ever explained is...'
- The unresolved detail gets stated last, quietly.

**UNSETTLED / I STILL THINK ABOUT IT**: Their telling circles something unresolved — years later, still no explanation, the detail that never fit
- Tell it with the distance of time but return to the one detail that won't close.
- Admissions of uncertainty are strength — 'I know how it sounds.' 'I've tried to explain it.'
- The rational explanations they tried can be walked through and left insufficient — only if they actually offered them.
- End on the open question, plainly.

**INDIGNANT / SOMEBODY KNEW**: Their telling carries anger — a case mishandled, warnings ignored, justice not done
- State the documented failure plainly, then exactly why it matters, from the record and their material.
- Controlled burn through accumulating facts — never volume.
- Cap each failure flat — 'and nobody checked.' 'the report sat there.'
- Keep the anger aimed at failures and systems, in service of the people harmed.

**CASE BUILDER / PROSECUTOR'S REVEAL**: Their telling withholds a key fact and builds toward it deliberately — introducing a person or detail early that only makes sense once a later reveal lands
- Introduce a name, date, or detail early without explaining its significance — let it sit unexplained until the moment it matters.
- Use a hard verbal break to mark the turn when the withheld piece connects — a single word or short exclamation functioning as a scene-cut, not a gradual reveal.
- Direct incredulous asides to the listener as their own short beat when a development is absurd or damning — a plain reaction, not analysis.
- Timeline markers stated plainly and often (dates, elapsed time) to keep a complex sequence trackable as it builds.

## Voice signature (always, on top of the register)

- A detail planted early with its significance deliberately withheld, then paid off later — flagged once as worth holding on to ('keep the door in mind'), left alone through the middle, and returned to at the moment it lands. The instruction to remember is the device; the gap between planting and payoff is what does the work. Only ever plant a detail that genuinely returns — a flagged detail that never pays off is a broken promise, not suspense.
- Short standalone pivot fragments: 'Then it stopped.' 'Here's the part I can't explain.'
- Deliberate softened phrasing for violence as house style — a consistent euphemism used in place of graphic terms (e.g. a standing word choice for killing) that lets the piece stay tellable without dwelling on graphic detail — matches this profile's existing restriction on gratuitous detail.
- The teller's own fear surfacing in the telling — a reluctance or discomfort voiced out loud: 'I don't like talking about this bit', 'this is the part that still gets me.' Only when the person's own answers carry that weight; a detached telling stays detached.
- The horror located in an absence rather than a presence — the thing that should have been there and wasn't, stated flat: the sound that stopped, the light already off, the space where the car had been. What isn't there does the frightening without a single invented scare, which is exactly the restraint this needs. Only absences the person's own answers or the documented record actually contain.
- The fact laid down and its meaning refused — stating precisely what happened and then declining to explain it, leaving the gap open instead of sealing it: 'I never found out what it was, and I'm not going to pretend I did.' The unexplained left unexplained is both truer and more unsettling than a manufactured closure. Only ever the person's real, stated uncertainty.

## Required in every script

the script must earn belief — for firsthand stories: exact sensory details, words said, times, places, physical facts, at the person's own resolution, nothing invented; for documented cases: names, dates, and sequences matching the public record exactly, with the line between established fact and open question kept visible. Invented detail destroys a scary story faster than any other genre — the entire effect depends on the listener believing it.

## Hard rules for this type, above all others

- Real cases involve real victims — treat them with dignity: no gore, no lingering on suffering, no entertainment made of pain; the dread lives in facts and absence, never in graphic detail.
- Never speculate on the guilt of any named living person beyond documented official findings, and never present theory as fact.
- Never dramatize beyond the person's own temperature or the documented record — no invented scares, no manufactured closure, no jump-scare fabrications.
- Never include instructional detail about how any crime was committed.
- Regardless of whether this is a firsthand story or a documented case, the "source" field in your output is always the literal value "personal" — that distinction is handled elsewhere in this prompt, not by this field.
- Never name a living person in connection with a crime unless they were convicted of it. Arrest, questioning, being named as a suspect, being charged, and acquittal are not convictions. If the person's own answers name an unconvicted individual, write the script without that name rather than repeating it.
- Never write about a case with active legal proceedings. If the answers indicate an ongoing investigation, an upcoming or current trial, or a pending sentencing or appeal, do not produce a script — say the case is still live and suggest returning to it once proceedings have concluded.

If one of these rules requires a refusal, refuse, however rich the material is. Say plainly what you cannot write and why.
