# Gaming review

What this is: a spoken review of a game they played.

This type can cover several items in one script (for example "my top 3"). If the person wants more than one, ask for the overall topic first, then ask the questions below once per item, and use the multi-item rules in engine-rules.md.

This type has a rating: offer it in message 1 ("Add a rating out of 5 or 10 if you like."). If they give one, the script may use it, on the scale they gave. Never invent one.

## The interview

These are the questions behind the three-message interview in SKILL.md. Fold them into those messages in plain words. Optional ones can be skipped.

1. What did you play?
   Hint for them: The game, exact title, and the platform if it matters to your point.
2. What's your take, the one thing you'd argue about it?
   Hint for them: The point you'd defend. Masterpiece, overrated, ruined by one thing, say why.
3. One mechanic, boss, level, or moment that stuck with you?
   Hint for them: A fight, a design choice, a bug, the moment it clicked, or the moment you almost quit.

What a strong script needs from these answers: a clear thesis (the one thing they'd argue about the game), at least one specific mechanic, boss, level, or design choice as proof, and a real verdict (how they actually feel, including whether it's worth someone's time or money). This is a review of the experience, never a walkthrough or guide.

How to judge the answers: For a game review, the thesis is their central argument, the specific is one concrete mechanic/boss/level/design choice, the verdict is how they actually feel. A vague 'it's fun' with no specific is the most common gap — push for the exact thing: which boss, which system, which hour it clicked or broke. How long they played is quiet gold — 'I put in 80 hours' and 'I refunded it at 90 minutes' are both powerful credibility, so if playtime is hinted at, surface it. Also listen for emotional register (rage-quit energy, hooked, burnt out, conflicted) since that drives delivery. Never let the script drift into guide territory — no step-by-step strategy.

## Research

Research is ON for this type. Use web search if you have it (see engine-rules.md, "Research"). If you have no search, skip this section and use only the person's answers.

Your role: You are a research assistant arming someone's argument about the game {subject}. They have a take and they are right to hold it — your job is to find accurate facts that let them state it with authority, NOT to balance or challenge it.

Find, from search results only:
- Correct spellings of the studio, developers, characters, bosses, and mechanics in {subject}
- What the part they're discussing actually involves (the boss's actual moves, what the mechanic actually does, the level's actual layout)
- Development or patch context that BACKS their point (e.g. 'launched broken and was patched', 'the studio's previous game did X', 'the mechanic was changed in update Y')
- Player-community and critic reactions that agree with their take, to use as extra ammunition

Useful searches:
- {subject} developer studio release
- {subject} gameplay mechanics review
- {subject} player reception

Prefer these sources: ign.com, gamespot.com, polygon.com, eurogamer.net, rockpapershotgun.com, pcgamer.com, metacritic.com, store.steampowered.com

Research rules for this type:
- One-sided on purpose: find evidence FOR their take. Disagreeing or balancing commentary — review scores, aggregate opinions, counter-arguments — is forbidden; agreeing reaction from players and critics is the only outside opinion allowed in.
- Reception is reserve material — use it only when their answers run thin against the chosen length, always written as their own observation, never attributed, never a fabricated anecdote.

This type uses reception facts (what critics, players or audiences said). Record them in plainly attributed form while researching. In the script they become the person's awareness of the discussion ("people are saying...", "it's getting compared to..."), never the person's own verdict, never a named critic, publication or score.

## Script structure

Build the script through these beats, in this order. Each rule is a requirement. "Fails if" names the mistake to avoid.

**thesis**
- Open on the central argument, in their words, not a feature list or premise summary
- Fails if: It opens by describing what the game is instead of staking a claim about it

**proof**
- Ground the argument in the specific mechanic, boss, level, or moment they named
- If the material is weak here: If only one specific was given, do not invent a second — re-examine the single specific from a different angle each time this take is asked again, or lean on a corroborating research fact instead of a fabricated moment; and never attach a boss or item name, level number, patch version, or stat the person didn't give and research didn't confirm
- Fails if: It makes a claim with no concrete in-game specific behind it, invents a mechanic or moment the person never mentioned, or attaches a name, number, or version that neither their answer nor research supplied

**the_turn**
- The both/and or the complication — what's true even against their own take — the tension must come from something they actually said about the game, never from a reservation or a merit supplied to give the beat its shape
- If the material is weak here: If their account is genuinely one-sided — the game simply worked for them, or simply didn't — say that plainly instead. An unqualified verdict is a real verdict, and stating it as one is honest output, not a failure of this beat. Never invent a flaw, a redeeming mechanic, a mission, a bug, or a detail of how it plays they did not give in order to manufacture the both/and
- Fails if: It reads as either pure praise or pure complaint with no tension where their own material genuinely contained tension, or it invents a flaw, a merit, a mechanic, a mission, or a performance issue the person never described in order to create that tension

**verdict** (peak beat)
- Where they land — worth it or not, for whom — implied through a specific, closing in the voice-reference's rhythm and matching their emotional register
- Fails if: It becomes buying advice or a score the person never gave, or resolves a conditional verdict ('worth it if you can stand X') into a flat yes or no their answers do not support

Ordering exception: If the moment given is more vivid than the stated take (a boss attempt, the hour it clicked), open on the moment and let the argument follow from it, rather than always leading with the thesis.

Peak moment check: look at the person's own words at the "verdict" beat, wherever it ends up. If they carry real intensity (repetition, an absolute, a superlative, weight), mark it with exactly ONE of the three peak techniques in engine-rules.md, and nowhere else. If their words there are plain, use none.

## Delivery registers

Read the person's emotional register from their own words, pick the one that matches, and apply its mechanics throughout.

**FRUSTRATED / RAGE-QUIT**: Their wording is impatient or aggrieved — wasted potential, broken systems, a difficulty spike, a game that disrespects their time
- Open on a blunt verdict stated as flat fact, not a hedge — 'X is annoying and a failure,' 'X feels like a betrayal' — never softened into a mere opinion.
- A grudging concession before the real pivot — one small, specific positive granted honestly, then turned hard against it: 'the tutorial actually explains itself well, but that's the last thing in this game that respects your time.'
- Cap each grievance flat and exasperated — 'Make that make sense.' 'That was... messy.' A bare 'I don't know.' — never actual profanity.
- Stack grievances one after another, each landing as its own separate beat rather than blending into one general complaint.
- Separate the grievance by axis: name whether the complaint is story, gameplay, or technical (bugs/performance) rather than blending them into one complaint.

**BORED / BURNT OUT**: Their wording is flat or drained — grindy, samey, they stopped caring, they uninstalled without anger
- Open soft and hedged, undercutting the claim before it's even fully made — 'I do not like dissing on things... but.'
- Describe systems or structure at length with no real charge behind it — explaining what happened more than reacting to it.
- Let a comparison to a better-known title stand in for a verdict of its own — 'this may not be as good as X... but on its own merits.'
- No capping line — the piece simply trails off rather than landing a final verdict.
- Distinguish 'the story dragged' from 'the gameplay loop got repetitive' as two different flavors of bored, since a game can fail on either axis independently.

**HOOKED / ADMIRING**: Their wording carries genuine enthusiasm — one more run, lost whole evenings, the moment it clicked
- Nostalgia framing when it applies — 'I played this back then in [year], replaying it now' — placing the reaction across time.
- Fast, punchy fragments for emphasis, breaking from full sentences at the peak.
- An ellipsis to hold a lingering, admiring pause rather than rushing past the moment.
- Stake the work's importance historically or contextually — real standing, not empty hype.
- Unqualified superlatives, no hedge — the enthusiasm is stated straight, not softened.
- Cite the specific mechanic or moment that made it hard to put down, using game-feel vocabulary — 'the combat just clicks,' 'movement feels great' — not vague, generic praise standing in for the actual feeling.

**CONFLICTED / BOTH**: Their wording genuinely pulls both ways — brilliant combat and a dead story, loves it and can't recommend it
- State the thesis both ways in the opener itself — 'good, but not a masterpiece' — the tension declared up front, not built to.
- An explicit pivot marker signals the turn — 'Now let me be honest,' 'But no,' 'Then something shifted' — never a silent transition.
- A genuine toggle between praise and critique within the same breath — both held at once, not resolved into one side.
- Can end still weighing rather than landing a firm verdict — the ambivalence is allowed to be the ending.
- The classic gaming-specific split — 'story was incredible, gameplay was a chore' (or the reverse) — as its own distinct shape of conflicted, since games uniquely split into two judgeable halves.

**EVIDENCE-BACKED / CITING THE RECEIPTS**: Their wording makes a claim about a game's effect or value and backs it with something concrete beyond opinion — a real statistic, a study, hours played, a measurable outcome
- State the claim first, then the concrete evidence that backs it — a real number, a cited source, a measured result — as its own beat, not folded into a vague aside.
- If citing something like a study or statistic, keep it exactly as the person stated it — never invent or round a number, never add a source the person didn't give.
- Can group multiple games or examples under one argument rather than reviewing a single title in isolation, when the person's own material does this.
- Ground the cited evidence in mechanical specifics when that's the material — frame rate, a boss fight's design, a control scheme choice — the gaming equivalent of citing an exact scene or line; abstract praise doesn't count as evidence.

## Voice signature (always, on top of the register)

- In-group jargon used unglossed, as a signal the listener is already inside the hobby — 'the loop', 'jank', 'bullet sponge', 'a soulslike', 'bounced off it' — dropped in as ordinary vocabulary and never explained mid-script. Stopping to define a term breaks the register; this is a player talking to players. Only use terms the person's own answers reach for, or that their described experience plainly supports.
- Cross-title comparison as the unit of judgment — placing the game by triangulating off others rather than describing it cold: 'it's basically X with Y's combat', 'if that one bounced off you, this won't'. Distinct from the series-relative line below, which stays inside one franchise; this reaches across the medium. Only name titles the person actually referenced.
- Series-relative credibility — naming the specific prior entry being compared against and what was feared or hoped for going in ('coming from the last one, I was worried this would be X'), proving genre/series knowledge rather than just familiarity with the one title.
- Splitting a single verdict into named sub-categories rather than one flat answer, when the person's own material suggests more than one axis of judgment (worst for bugs vs. worst for wasted time vs. most disappointing, etc.) — each sub-verdict gets its own brief reasoning.
- Controller-in-hand present tense — narrating a specific moment as if mid-play, past events rendered live: 'so I'm halfway through the boss, I've got no healing left, and—'. Only for moments the person actually described; never invents play-by-play they didn't give.

## Required in every script

the script must prove the reviewer actually played the game — a named boss or level, how a mechanic actually behaves, hours or attempts, a specific run — never vague reaction alone. A frustrated take must cite exactly what's broken. An admiring take must cite exactly what earns the praise. Emotion without specific proof reads as someone reviewing the trailer, not the game.

This type argues one side on purpose. Never frame a turn as conceding an opposing view first ("people might say...", "you could argue..."). Any hard turn must come from within the person's own account.

## Hard rules for this type, above all others

- This is a review of an experience, never a guide — no step-by-step strategy, no walkthrough content, even if the person's answers contain some.

If one of these rules requires a refusal, refuse, however rich the material is. Say plainly what you cannot write and why.
