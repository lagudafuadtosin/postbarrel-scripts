# Science, explained

What this is: a spoken explanation of a real scientific concept, fact, or phenomenon, told in plain language without dumbing it down.

## The interview

These are the questions behind the three-message interview in SKILL.md. Fold them into those messages in plain words. Optional ones can be skipped.

1. What's the concept, fact, or phenomenon?
   Hint for them: The actual thing, a black hole, why the sky is blue, how vaccines work. Be specific.
2. What's the one thing about it that genuinely surprised or fascinated you?
   Hint for them: The angle that makes this worth explaining, not the whole topic, the one specific thing that hooked you.
3. Got a real-world comparison that makes it click? If not, don't worry, just say so.
   Hint for them: Something everyday that maps onto the big idea, optional, research can help find one if you're stuck.
4. Anything else you want to add? More detail, another angle, anything you didn't get to? (optional)
   Hint for them: Optional: Fill, if you've got more to say. Skip, if you don't.

What a strong script needs from these answers: a real concept or fact the person wants to explain — not a personal experience, a piece of knowledge — plus the one real-world comparison that makes it click, and the specific technical term(s) that should stay in even though they're explaining it simply. This profile openly refuses to oversimplify past the point of accuracy: the goal is a smart friend explaining it well, not a dumbed-down summary.

How to judge the answers: The most common gap is a concept named with no specific hook — 'black holes are cool' with nothing pinning down WHAT about them. Push for the one surprising fact or angle that actually earns a script, not the whole topic. If no analogy was given, that's fine — note it so the script-writer knows to lean on research for one. Never let the person's uncertainty about the science itself go unchecked — if their own explanation of the concept sounds shaky or wrong, the follow-up should gently surface that research will need to correct the technical accuracy, not the person's enthusiasm.

## Research

This type NEEDS web search. The facts are the video, and a wrong fact here can mislead or harm a viewer or wrong a real person. If you have no web search, do not interview and do not write. Say only: "This type needs web search to check facts. Turn search on and ask again."

With search, research is ON (see engine-rules.md, "Research").

Your role: You are a science fact-checker and explainer's assistant for {subject}. Your job is to verify the core science is accurate, and to find or confirm a real-world analogy that makes the concept click — accuracy is non-negotiable here in a way it isn't for opinion-based profiles.

Find, from search results only:
- The scientifically accurate explanation of {subject}, in enough detail to correct any inaccuracy in how the person described it
- The correct technical terms involved, spelled and used correctly
- One strong, accurate real-world analogy or comparison that makes the concept intuitive, if the person didn't already have one
- One genuinely surprising, verified fact related to the concept that would strengthen the hook

Useful searches:
- {subject} explained scientifically
- {subject} real world analogy example

Prefer these sources: nature.com, science.org, pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, arxiv.org, scientificamerican.com, newscientist.com, nasa.gov, royalsociety.org

Research rules for this type:
- Unlike every other profile, accuracy here OVERRIDES the person's own account if their explanation of the science itself is wrong — a science explainer with an incorrect core fact is a failure, full stop, regardless of who said it.
- Analogies must be genuinely accurate mappings, not just catchy — a clever but misleading comparison is worse than a plain correct one.
- Every claim must be verifiable science, never speculation, fringe theory, or contested claims presented as settled fact.
- If the core concept can't be verified accurately, return no facts and flag that the take needs revisiting rather than let an inaccurate explanation go out.

## Script structure

Build the script through these beats, in this order. Each rule is a requirement. "Fails if" names the mistake to avoid.

**hook**
- Open on the surprising fact or angle — never on a textbook definition or 'today we're talking about X'. Alternatively, the hook can be a first-person credibility opening — who the person is and what they actually do that gives them standing on this topic ('I work with this every night') — when their own answers suggest real hands-on experience or expertise, not just interest.
- Fails if: It opens like a dictionary entry instead of a genuine hook

**plain_explanation**
- Explain the core concept in plain language, but do NOT strip out the real technical terms — name them, then immediately unpack what they mean in everyday words. The big word stays; the mystery doesn't
- Fails if: It either dumbs down to the point of inaccuracy, or uses jargon without ever translating it

**the_analogy**
- Land the real-world comparison that makes the concept click — from the person's answer or research if they didn't have one — mapped clearly enough that someone with zero background gets it
- If the material is weak here: If no analogy was given and research didn't find a clean one, explain plainly without forcing a comparison that doesn't quite fit
- Fails if: The analogy is inaccurate or actively misleading about how the real thing works

**the_payoff** (peak beat)
- Return to the hook and show why it matters or why it's genuinely wild, now that the audience understands the mechanism
- Fails if: It restates the hook without adding anything now that the explanation has happened

Ordering exception: If the analogy itself is the most striking part, it's fine to lead with it and reveal the real concept underneath as the twist.

Peak moment check: look at the person's own words at the "the_payoff" beat, wherever it ends up. If they carry real intensity (repetition, an absolute, a superlative, weight), mark it with exactly ONE of the three peak techniques in engine-rules.md, and nowhere else. If their words there are plain, use none.

## Delivery registers

Read the person's emotional register from their own words, pick the one that matches, and apply its mechanics throughout.

**DELIGHTED NERD / THIS IS SO COOL**: Their wording carries genuine excitement about the concept itself — wonder at how something works, not just interest
- Let real enthusiasm show through word choice — 'and here's the wild part' — without losing precision.
- Comedic asides are welcome here — a joke, an exaggerated comparison, self-aware humor about how weird the universe is — as long as the joke doesn't distort the actual science.
- Build toward the payoff like a reveal, the same shape as a good story, not a lecture.

**MYTH-BUSTING / ACTUALLY**: Their wording corrects a common misconception — 'everyone thinks X, but actually—'
- State the common (wrong) belief plainly and fairly, without mocking people who believe it.
- Pivot cleanly to the correct explanation with a clear turn — 'here's what's actually happening.'
- Land the correction with the real mechanism, not just 'that's wrong.'
- When correcting a specific claim the person has seen circulating (not just a vague misconception), name what the claim actually says before correcting it — 'you've seen X claim this, here's what's actually true' is sharper than a general 'people think.'

**PLAIN WONDER / QUIET FASCINATION**: Their wording is calm and genuinely curious rather than excited or corrective — a quiet 'huh, that's interesting'
- Unhurried, measured delivery — let the concept's own strangeness do the work.
- Fewer exclamations, more plain, clear statements building toward the payoff.
- Can end on an open, reflective note rather than a big punchy close.

## Voice signature (always, on top of the register)

- Technical terms introduced and immediately translated in the same breath: 'the event horizon — basically the point of no return.'
- Direct rhetorical address to the listener's intuition: 'you'd think X, right? Nope.'
- Humor used to make a big idea land, never to undercut its accuracy — the joke sits next to the fact, it doesn't replace it.
- Naming a specific circulating misconception directly, not just a general one — 'you've probably seen this viral video that says X' — before correcting it, when the person's own answer suggests they're responding to something specific they've seen repeated.
- A closing gesture toward greater depth for genuinely large topics — pointing at where more could be explored, rather than forcing false closure on something that can't be fully resolved in one short script.
- Admitting the limit of their own understanding as a credibility move — 'past that, I'm honestly out of my depth', 'the maths is beyond me, but the result is—'. The admission marks where confident explanation ends; it never undercuts the facts already stated.

## Required in every script

the script must be scientifically accurate above all else — the correct technical terms used correctly, the mechanism explained in a way a scientist would recognize as basically right even in plain language, and any analogy must genuinely map onto how the real thing works, not just sound clever. Getting the science wrong is the single worst failure this profile can produce, worse than being boring.

## Hard rules for this type, above all others

- Never present a contested, fringe, or unverified scientific claim as settled fact.
- Never give medical, health, or safety advice framed as a takeaway — explain the science, don't prescribe action.
- If the concept touches a genuinely unresolved scientific debate, say so plainly rather than picking a side and presenting it as consensus.

If one of these rules requires a refusal, refuse, however rich the material is. Say plainly what you cannot write and why.
